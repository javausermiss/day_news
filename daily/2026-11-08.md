Agents can retrieve documents and query databases, but reliability gates agentic analytics: a revenue metric answered faster is worth nothing if the number is wrong. Agents need a context layer that tells them where data lives, what it means, whether to use it, how to use it, where it came from, and what is similar. NVIDIA Auto Ontology is an open library that builds that layer from enterprise metadata, reconciles definitions across systems, and exposes it to any agent harness through MCP, CLI, and API. It complements the semantic models customers already own.
It enables developers to:
- Discover: generate enterprise ontologies from metadata; connect existing ontologies and semantic models; build AI-ready knowledge graphs.
- Ground: give agents trusted business meaning for metrics, entities, and relationships; reconcile inconsistent definitions across systems.
- Validate: check generated queries against certified definitions and lineage; return provenance with every answer.
- Expose: serve context to any agent harness through MCP, CLI, and API. Learn: fold certified corrections and usage back into the ontology, so shared context improves with every question.
To run a text-to-SQL benchmark such as BIRD, refer to the ontology SQL evaluation repository.

Rails is a web application framework that includes everything needed to create database-backed web applications according to the Model-View-Controller (MVC) pattern.
Understanding the MVC pattern is key to understanding Rails. MVC divides your application into three layers: Model, View, and Controller, each with a specific responsibility.
The Model layer represents the domain model (such as Account, Product,
Person, Post, etc.) and encapsulates the business logic specific to
your application. In Rails, database-backed model classes are derived from
ActiveRecord::Base. Active Record allows you to present the data from
database rows as objects and embellish these data objects with business logic
methods.
Although most Rails models are backed by a database, models can also be ordinary
Ruby classes, or Ruby classes that implement a set of interfaces as provided by
the Active Model module.
The View layer is composed of "templates" that are responsible for providing appropriate representations of your application's resources. Templates can come in a variety of formats, but most view templates are HTML with embedded Ruby code (ERB files). Views are typically rendered to generate a controller response or to generate the body of an email. In Rails, View generation is handled by Action View.
The Controller layer is responsible for handling incoming HTTP requests and
providing a suitable response. Usually, this means returning HTML, but Rails controllers
can also generate XML, JSON, PDFs, mobile-specific views, and more. Controllers load and
manipulate models, and render view templates in order to generate the appropriate HTTP response.
In Rails, incoming requests are routed by Action Dispatch to an appropriate controller, and
controller classes are derived from ActionController::Base. Action Dispatch and Action Controller
are bundled together in Action Pack.
Active Record, Active Model, Action Pack, and Action View can each be used independently outside Rails.
In addition to that, Rails also comes with:
- Action Mailer, a library to generate and send emails
- Action Mailbox, a library to receive emails within a Rails application
- Active Job, a framework for declaring jobs and making them run on a variety of queuing backends
- Action Cable, a framework to integrate WebSockets with a Rails application
- Active Storage, a library to attach cloud and local files to Rails applications
- Action Text, a library to handle rich text content
- Active Support, a collection of utility classes and standard library extensions that are useful for Rails, and may also be used independently outside Rails

- A somewhat capable CPU. Hardware decoding might help if the CPU is too slow to
decode video in realtime, but must be explicitly enabled with the --hwdec option.
- A not too crappy GPU. mpv's focus is not on power-efficient playback on
embedded or integrated GPUs (for example, hardware decoding is not even
enabled by default). Low power GPUs may cause issues like tearing, stutter,
etc. On such GPUs, it's recommended to use --profile=fast for smooth playback.
The main video output uses shaders for video rendering and scaling,
rather than GPU fixed function hardware. On Windows, you might want to make
sure the graphics drivers are current. In some cases, ancient fallback video
output methods can help (such as --vo=xv on Linux), but this use is not
recommended or supported.

In 2023 after the ChatGPT release, it was clear to me that research needed to be more organized to succeed than was typical within Microsoft Research. The amount of support and focus which other research groups were applying was great enough that even with a clever idea pursued by one person it was likely to arrive late and unconvincing because one person could not move fast enough and the baselines which one person could assemble just were not strong enough to really show that an idea was worth taking flight. Several other folks thought similarly and we organized to create the AI Frontiers sub-organization within MSR.
In AI Frontiers, we actively tried to organize coherent research efforts. This was not precisely “top down” or “bottom up”, but rather mostly somewhere in the messy middle. There were disagreements of course, and resolving them was sometimes quite challenging. Compute availability was also an often-severe bottleneck. But at a high level I was under the general impression that the organization was succeeding in doing pioneering things, particularly given the small size of AIF (~50 people) and relative to what we could do previously. Much of the work of AIF was figuring out how to make agents work, for example the Autogen orchestration framework and the Fara computer use agent. In NYC however, several of us were interested in developing new fundamental capabilities such as the Next Latent objective which enables extrapolation, Dion3 which creates an optimized/improved Muon, or the Full Bandwidth Transformer which efficiently makes models function substantially more powerfully via recursion.
We also actively tried to share with the rest of MSR. For example, AIF decided to develop a monorepo that was shared by default with all of Microsoft Research. In this monorepo for example was the proven-capable edge scale pretraining codebase I talked about in the COLM keynote last month. This might seem like a minor detail, but the amount effort required to create a well-formed model creation pipeline is substantial and I’m aware of at least 4 efforts (the other 3 not in public-within-MSR repos) trying to develop model building pipelines.
In early September we were reorged into Microsoft AI. On the surface this could make sense, because putting two different AI organizations together creates opportunities for better concentration of effort, and there were places where AIF was ahead of MAI and of course vice-versa (MAI is much larger). In practice, there are substantial issues because the philosophy of the ex-Inflection leadership of MAI is incompatible. The core philosophy there is scaling, and aggressively not research. This might shock some folks who naturally think that the way you make better models is via research but there is plenty of evidence that bigger models are better models as far as the quality of the predictions they make and from a business perspective returns to scale are far more predictable than returns to research. In a scaling-only environment of course the primary effort is on getting/making/refining data, which is quite a step away from what we were working on. Aside from the incompatible focus, there was plenty of evidence that they just did not really want us.
It’s a tough situation to find yourself in. I consider the scaling-only focus to be both misdirected and not fun. It is an understandable failure mode—scaling is straightforward and it’s common for people to give up on a tradeoff between multiple competing ideals (like innovation and size) by short-sightedly adopting one in totality and dropping the other. But returns to research are real and significant. For example, the Bytedance experiments suggest a variant of the Full Bandwidth Transformer might be comparable to a model of twice the size, implying significant savings in training and inference for the same capability. Do the math—how much is it worth to halve training and inference cost given that the industry has investments approaching a trillion dollars?
So I situated my folks as best I could and left. I am quite fortunate in that there are many other people in the world who are capable of seeing the value of research and innovation which is my intrinsic center of interest. Every other founding AIF leadership team member seems to have come to a similar conclusion.
This does leave a question: Why not go back to MSR? I chatted with folks a bit but could not get away from two serious obstacles. The first is that I am just not comfortable being at a company where the research I do has no path to real use. I could not get innovation into use (or even seriously considered) when I was in MAI, so the prospects for success from MSR seem quite small. The second is that I’m afraid there’s a breakdown of the basic deal in MSR: do good work and good things happen. As far as I can tell we did good work and were non-consensually reorged away. Unfortunately, I would not be surprised to see that kind of thing continue to happen until MSR leadership develops a will to effectively organize to meet the moment.
So AI Frontiers is over. I’m sorry to say that there will not be internships with the folks there in the coming year—I enjoyed working with interns on many occasions. And my best wishes for the folks I leave behind. It is not hard to imagine a Microsoft of the future which becomes attractive for research again if the people in control can develop a little bit more wisdom.

Back in the early ’90s, it wasn’t a sure thing that Microsoft Windows was going to take over the market, even though they had a clear lead over many of their competitors, thanks to MS-DOS.
In fact, one of the iconic GUI-based experiences of the era, AOL, hedged its bets for a while, creating and maintaining a DOS version of its iconic pseudo-internet software using a graphical user interface platform few were familiar with: GeoWorks.
It was an operating system for an era when it wasn’t even a sure thing we’d have a modem.
And it was absurdly lightweight, something it gained from its earliest form—as GEOS (Graphical Environment Operating System), an operating system option for the Commodore 64.
The platform, built by Berkeley Softworks—not to be confused with Berkeley Systems, which built the famous “flying toasters” screensaver—was one of the most popular pieces of software on Commodore 64 for a time, thanks to the fact that it was very functional and worked on very inexpensive hardware.
“GEOS did not pioneer the GUI; most of its features were already present in the larger OSes of the day, like the classic Mac (albeit, not Windows),” writer Kroc Camen wrote of GEOS for OS News back in 2006. “What GEOS did show is that cheap, low-power, commodity hardware and simple office productivity software worked. The operating system eventually moved to the PC in the early ’90s in a more advanced form, and Berkeley Softworks changed its name to GeoWorks.
I had some experience with Commodore 64 thanks to a childhood friend of mine who owned one and let me mess around with it a bit, but ultimately, I caught onto the PC version of GeoWorks because it came bundled with a 386 I used when I was a kid.

I've read the classics--Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs, Paradigms of Artificial Intelligence Programming--but I'd like to highlight some of the more esoteric books which affected my thinking.
Zen of Assembly Language
Michael Abrash, 1990
I spent much of the 1980s writing 8-bit computer games (which you can read about if you like). Odd as it may seem in retrospect, considering the relative power of an 8-bit 6502 running at sub 2 MHz, I wasn't obsessed with optimizing for performance. If anything, I wanted the code to be small, a side effect of using a line-based editor and writing programs that someone would have to painstakingly type in from a magazine listing. Pages of DATA 0AFF6CA900004021... ugh.
Right when that period of my life came to a close, along came Zen of Assembly Language, by an author I had never heard of, which dissected, explained, and extended the self-taught tricks from my years as a lone assembly hacker. Even though Abrash was focused on the 8086 and not the 6502, it felt like the book was written personally to me.
This is also one of the most bizarrely delayed technical book releases I can recall. The majority of the book was about detailed optimization for the 8088 and 8086, yet it was published when the 80486 was showing up in high-end desktop PCs.
Scientific Forth
Julian Noble, 1992
Fractals, Visualization, and J
Clifford Reiter, 2000
These two books are about entirely different subjects. One is about pure scientific computation. The other is about generating images. Each uses a different, wildly non-mainstream language for the included code.
And yet these two books follow the same general approach, one I wish were more commonly used. Superficially, the authors have written introductions to particular programming languages (which is why the language name is in the title). But in reality it's more that each author has an area of deep expertise and has found a language that enables experimenting with and writing code to solve problems in that field. As such, there aren't forced examples and toy problems, but serious, non-trivial programs that show a language in actual use. Dr. Noble demonstrates how he uses Forth for hardcore matrix work and, when he realizes that RPN notation isn't ideal in all circumstances, develops a translator from infix expressions to Forth. Clifford Reiter jumps into image processing algorithms, plus veers into lighter weight diversions with titles like "R/S Analysis, the Hurst Exponent, and Sunspots."
Both books are wonderful alternatives to the usual "Learning Programing Language of the Month" texts. Sadly, Julian Noble died in 2007.
Programmers At Work
Susan Lammers, 1986
I used to soak up printed interviews with programmers and game designers (and typically game designers were programmers as well). I was enthralled by Levy's Hackers, more the game development chapter than the rest. Programmers At Work was in the same vein: philosophies, ideas, and experiences directly from an odd mix of famous and quirky programmers. But the book wasn't primarily about tech. It was about creativity. Most of the people interviewed didn't have degrees in computer science. There wasn't an emphasis on math, proving programs correct, lambda calculus--just people coming up with ideas and implementing them. And the game connection was there: Jaron Lanier talking about the psychedelic Moon Dust for the Commodore 64, Bill Budge's bold (and still unfulfilled) plan to build a "Construction Set Construction Set."
Lammers's book was the model I used when I put together Halcyon Days. I also pulled Hackers into the mix by interviewing John Harris about his dissatisfaction with Levy's presentation of him. In an odd twist of fate, Programmers at Work and Halcyon Days were packaged together on a single CD sold through the Dr. Dobb's Journal library. The pairing has been around for ten years and is still available, much to my surprise.
Thinking Forth: A Language and Philosophy for Solving Problems
Leo Brodie, 1984
Yes, another book about Forth.
But this one is worth reading less for the Forth and more because it's one of the few books about how to decompose problems and structure code. You'd think this book was written by Fowler and friends, until you realize it's from the mid-1980s. That Brodie uses "factor" (which originated in the Forth community) instead of "refactor" is also a giveaway. What's impressive here is there's no OOP, no discussion of patterns, no heavy terminology. It's a book about understanding what you're trying to achieve, avoiding redundancy, and writing dead simple code.
It's worth it for the Forth, too, especially the interspersed bits of wisdom from experts, including Forth creator Chuck Moore.

即使是流行畅销书，读罢一本，也会或多或少在脑海中留下深刻的印记。是的，我想说，对我来说就是 Kindle 培养了我的阅读习惯，以一种方便的、唾手可得的方式给我种下了种子。高中之后我开始逐渐从流行文学读更多东西，读波拉尼奥、读福克纳，但 Kindle 就是一个起点。
给我震动最大的电子设备——没有之一——是索尼 DPT-RP1。我爸在它一发售就花了大几千块钱从日本海淘回来。这台设备至今在我见过的所有电子产品中，优雅程度排第一位。纯黑的薄如蝉翼的外观，宛若用铅笔在纸上的触感，开箱的一瞬间我仿佛在开一个来自外星球的产物。
我把这玩意带去英语课补习，别人都写在纸上，就我写在电子纸上，无敌吸引眼球。2020 年疫情来了，这台设备更是派上了大用场，我可以不打印作业，直接在 DPT-RP1 上写，在把 PDF 直接发给老师，感觉自己优雅得不行。
高中之后，一开始它只是屏幕上有一块坏死的区域没法写字，我只能用它看看书；后来设备的边角开始裂开，我才知道「薄如蝉翼」也有代价。再后来触控屏失灵，它只能躺在我书桌最底下的抽屉里，成为我少年时代的纪念品。
我与苹果
被 Windows Mobile 伤透了心之后，我爸投入了苹果的怀抱。在我上高中之前，每年的九月的某一天我起床吃早饭的时候，如果发现饭桌上我爸架着 iPad 在看昨晚的苹果发布会回放，我就知道苹果又出新品了。
iPhone 6 的金色配色被很多人称之为「土豪金」，我爸买了 iPhone 6 Plus 送给我妈，我记得我和我妈一起拆箱。我爸则买了一个黑色的小屏 iPhone 6，直到今天我都认为 iPhone 6 有着所有苹果手机中最优雅的外观。后来虽然我爸有淘汰下来 iPhone 6，但我却一点不想用它，苹果设备没法自由安装安装包，你不得不折腾各种各样的手机助手，我这种体会了安卓自由滋味的人永远不会满意。
我不喜欢苹果的封闭生态，但没有人会否认苹果的产品就是走在最前沿的，它在乎细节，有质感和格调。
虽然我爸用苹果手机比较晚，但我家很早就有了一台第三代 iPad，主要用来看美剧，当时搜狐还有很多美剧，我跟着我爸一块看，印象最深的就是《越狱》；初中的时候去闵行上学而思培优，它有一个 iPad APP，所以也需要用到 iPad。
iPad 上让我印象最深的游戏除了人尽皆知的《水果忍者》，还有《我的世界》故事模式，和《三国杀 烈》，一个剧情模式的《三国杀》更是我心里最好的《三国杀》电子游戏。《我的世界》故事模式由于制作商倒闭了彻底绝版，而《三国杀 烈》也是除了 iPad 没有上架过任何平台，并且由于只支持 32 位，今天的 iPad 也无法运行了。
2022 年我不得不在家上网课，听说当时的 MacBook 换了 M 系列芯片，我颇感好奇。我爸想着 MacBook 又不能打游戏就给我买了一个。
macOS 还是可以自由安装软件的，因此这是唯一一台让我爱不释手的苹果设备。我很快就发现苹果电脑的屏幕素质实在是太好了，上网课的时候天天挂着 OBS 虚拟摄像头，然后窝在房间里看电影。
不知道什么原因，B 站当时开始大买版权，首页又一次给我推荐了戈达尔的《精疲力尽》，我人生中第一次看到和小时候在家里 IPTV 点播好莱坞电影截然不同的作品，就是在高二下和高三上这两个学期在家的时间。我就这么在上网课的日子里看了大概有一百部文艺片，从戈达尔到伯格曼，从费里尼到塔可夫斯基。结果当然是我的春考一塌糊涂。
我与微软
我是 Surface 系列的忠实粉丝，这主要是受我老爸的感染，他是微软 Lumia 坚守到最后一批的用户，初中给我买的第一台电脑是 Surface Book2（但是没有独显）。上了大学之后，我选择了 Surface Laptop Studio 作为我的笔记本电脑。
只是我越来越受不了 Windows，我把它的系统换成了 Linux。说真的，抛开性价比不谈，微软的整体外观就是 PC 阵营里最有高级感的。银色 logo 帅到爆炸。
Surface Duo 刚出的时候毫无疑问成为了我的白月光，大一的时候我终于在生活费里东抠西抠以三千多的价格搞来了一部 Surface Duo2，圆了自己高中的梦想。
我为了想要更好地使用，甚至把自己的笔记流程转移到了 OneNote。最后发现 OneNote 是一个被低估的好软件，但 Surface Duo 绝对是微软出过最失败的硬件之一。Surface Book 系列和 Surface Laptop Studio 都至少让我能够在看剧和电影的时候忽视键盘——Surface Duo 除了概念什么也没剩下。
微软试图以生产力工具的方式定义 Surface Duo，但是手机根本不是生产力工具。在绝大多数的情况下根本不需要同时双屏，所以并非折叠屏的它，就不得不面对那个雷霆大裂缝，更别说它又宽又厚，单手使用体验差得要命。
大一升大二的那个暑假，我和朋友去安吉漂流的时候把这个手机掉进了水里，它死得非常彻底。无论如何，我还是为 Panos Panay 时代的落幕而感到感伤。我自知他治下的每一款产品都难以称得上成功，但是我就是喜欢那些产品的 fanciness 本身，那种与众不同的个性，那种科技超脱工具本身的感觉。
今天的设备
大学之后有了些闲钱，我可以尝试各种各样的手机。除了 Surface Duo2，我用过一段时间的索尼 Xperia Pro-I，觉得它是带屏幕的黑卡，却发现自己根本不怎么拍照。
我后来尝试用过 Fairphone5，在解 BL 锁的时候出了一些问题然后发了邮件，过了 3 个月才收到第一封模板邮件回复，对于这样的神人客服我真的不知道说什么。更离谱的是不知道为什么我用着用着这个手机的 NFC 坏了。有趣的是尽管 Fairphone 的优点在于可维修性，NFC 坏了只需要换一个电池，但我当时在 UCB 交流，美国根本找不到 Fairphone 5 的备件。
我现在的手机是一台 Minimal Phone，大三在伯克利交流的时候刚好它开始预售发货，而好巧不巧我的 Fairphone 5 当时坏了。所以 Minimal Phone 一跃从玩具变成了主力机。
Minimal Phone 作为一款墨水屏手机其实完全足以日用，虽然我根本不会想用它去浏览任何需要滑动的社交媒体，但它也确实无论是用微信小程序点餐拿外卖，还是用来回微信和 QQ 消息都没毛病。有 NFC 可以 Google Pay 也可以支付宝碰一碰。它完美地卡在了你可以使用但又不想主动使用的区间。在地铁上的时候我会发呆，吃饭的时候我就只能吃饭，我觉得其实也挺好的。我根本没有想到，我手里的这部 Minimal Phone 我已经用了一年了，对我的 Doomscrolling 还真是给彻底根治了。
不过由于它的相机真的特别特别差——是真的连扫码都费劲，距离一远就别想企业微信签到了，而且缺少彩光有一些人脸识别的问题。所以最好有一部备机使用，比如我就有一个 root 了的小米 9。
科技应该是什么样
面对在 Reddit 这个广为传播并引起广泛共鸣的帖子，我不知道该作何感想。因为我永远也没法想象一个科技停止在这里的时间线。我没有办法想象自己没经历过的事物。
出生于 2005 年，我是移动互联网的原住民，我的整个青少年时期就是移动互联网在中国的普及史，我见证了我的小姑给我妈推荐微信这个软件，然后我妈买了一台小米 2S 作为智能机；我见证了家族里的亲戚们开始拥有拉起微信群，在过年玩抢红包和集五福；见证了我的完全不识字的奶奶居然学会了使用抖音，并且热衷于在上面看各种儿孙不孝剧情。
但某种程度上它的确写出了我们的心声，科技并不意味着让我们的生活变好。对我来说，电脑肯定是好的设备，电纸书肯定是好的设备，而智能手机或许不是。AI 呢？我说不准。
2021 年的时候我高二，看了奈飞的纪录片 The Social dilemma，我非常受触动，发了 QQ 空间转发给我的很多朋友。当初我的一些同学也同样深受感触的评论我说想要研究怎么拒绝手机和社交媒体。大家还是拥抱了社交媒体。
我的好哥们 Andy，初中的时候用最爆的语言批评抖音，跟大家说点一个赞刷一分钟，然后在帖子下面说自己刷不下去。每次和他出门吃饭的时候他都在看抖音。
我曾经是一个数码爱好者，天天刷 IT 之家、订电脑报、听李如一的《一天世界》播客，甚至还充了一天世界的会员计划。今天李如一早就淡出了中国互联网，看今天他的部落格里那种对于科技的冷感，你根本无法想象他是那个做《IT 公论》的李如一。我自高中之后再也没有看过电脑报或者 IT 之家。
我在 B 站还能刷到极客湾、TESTV，但我很多时候甚至连懒得点开；极客湾转型面向了手机评测，TESTV 和佼佼流口水分家。我曾经受何同学的影响想一边上大学一边做数码博主，最后我没有这么做，但我并不为此感到遗憾。对于今天的互联网来说为人所知或许并不是一件好事，何同学从数码博主转型做 DIY 之后我再也没有看过他的一期视频。
跋
今年大四，面临人生十字路口的我，选择在一个深夜一口气写下这些文字，或许是因为作为一名计算机专业的学生我有着极大的概率会进入互联网行业，而我只是想思考清楚科技对于我意味着什么。
事实上，高考之后我选择计算机专业不是因为乔布斯和马斯克的故事，更不是因为看过《社交网络》的电影，恰恰相反，是因为 Tim Berners-Lee，是因为读过 RMS 的传记《若为自由故》，是因为 Alan Swartz 的纪录片《互联网之子》。
无论今天的互联网让我感受如何，我都仍然有互联网的乌托邦情结。大概是在我初次邂逅互联网的少年年代，在贴吧、在 QQ 群，大家用网盘和群文件来分享资源，有一批人手把手不厌其烦的教授安装和使用软件：我知道了手机上要用 ZAchiver 来解压 7z，学会了在手机上如何安装谷歌框架，学会了在贴吧靠与佛论禅和与熊论道获取链接。
我在各种奇怪的地方获取影视资源，看到人们说 1024，好人一生平安。
在那个移动互联网方兴未艾的时代，它给了我一个可以钻进去的地下室，其间的感受不足为外人道也，但却使得那些孤独时光并不难捱。我知道那些新奇感并不完全属于那个时代，我想并不叙述某种什么过往黄金时代的陈词滥调。
我只想回应自己的感受，自己的记忆，只是因为那时候我满腔少年心气，对一切都充满好奇，对未来充满憧憬。

既然新老基础款 MacBook Pro 的核心区别就是 SoC 升级，那我就在这个部分多讲两句。
M6 采用了新的架构设计，从之前的 10 核 CPU、10 核 GPU 变成了 12 核 CPU、12 核 GPU，而 CPU 架构从之前的 4 个超级核心加 6 个能效核心变成了 2 个超级核心、4 个性能核心和 6 个能效核心。同时，M6 比 M5 多了一个神经网络引擎（Neural-engine），在处理 AI 运算时的理论速度更快。此外，M6 的内存带宽也从 153GB/s 提升到了最高 170GB/s，对于运行本地 AI 大模型更为友好。
从市面上各家的性能测试来看，M6 比 M5 的 CPU 单核性能提升了大约 10%、多核 20%，GPU 提升大约 20% 到 30%，NPU 则约 40% 到 50%。在实际的使用中，我也结合我的常用工具，针对性地做了对比。
这里要先提一点：我的 M5 版 MacBook Pro 是 16GB 内存的最基础款，但 M6 版则升级到了 32GB。更大的内存在一些项目里带来了更多优势，具体表现会在对应的测试里说明。
我首先尝试的是播客剪辑，使用《Reaper》渲染一个由两轨人声组成的对谈，全长超过一个半小时，且挂满了dxRevive Pro、VOTT 和 RX 系列插件，在播客项目里已经是比较重度的剪法。
播客渲染主要考验 CPU 部分的性能，M5 用时超过 15 分钟，M6 则只用 10 分钟就结束了战斗，领先优势甚至超过了通过跑分得到的理论数值。
第二项是使用《LM Studio》运行本地大模型，模型为 Gemma 4 12B。
两者的模型载入速度区别不大，都在 20 秒左右；token 的吐出速度 M6 略优，为每秒 19 左右，M5 则为每秒 16 左右。在这个项目里，M6 更高的内存带宽想必发挥了一定作用。
这里就谈到了前面提到的内存容量问题。我的最初计划是使用较新的 Qwen3.8 27B 模型进行对比，但无奈 16GB 的 M5 直接被客户端禁止加载，于是才被迫改用了更小的模型。其实 32GB 的电脑不仅可以顺利加载 Qwen3.8 27B，而且 token 生成速度为每秒 10 个左右，还多了推理能力，在真实场景里会更实用。
这正是 AI 为大内存赋予的新意义——更大的统一内存有机会容纳体积更大的本地模型，干活的质量和效率也得以更佳。如果你需要在电脑上长期运行 OpenClaw 之类的 AI 代理服务，那把钱花在大内存上，选一个品质更高的本地模型，长期来看可能更加划算。
接下来回到性能对比，下一个项目是《ComfyUI》文生图，我使用 Flux 2 base 4B 模型，生成一张 1024*1024 的图片。M6 从首次加载模型到出图用时 5 分 50 秒左右，M5 则需要 8 分钟。两者速度都不算快，相比之下，M6 仍节省了不少时间。
再下一项是使用《Whisper Transcription》将一段不到 12 分钟的英文 YouTube 影片转成文字，模型选择 app 自带的 Large V3 Turbo。这是我测试 Apple silicon 的经典项目，《Whisper Transcription》会同时调用 GPU 和 NPU，而且非常依赖 NPU 能效；语音转文字又是我的常用场景，所以既能展示性能差异，也有现实意义。M6 用时为 24 秒 26，从文件拖入之后就开始出字，非常迅捷；M5 则用时 41 秒 14，明显慢了非常多。
更下一项是用《Topaz Photo》的自动模式（Auto pilot）模式将 10 张低分辨率猫猫图自动优化为高分图片，M6 用时 10 秒 07，M5 则要用时 20 秒 58。《Topaz Photo》主要调用 GPU 和 NPU，所以 M6 赢得也很明显。
最后也是最重度的测试，使用《Davinci Resolve》进行视频渲染。我直接选择了刚刚发布的新片《iPhone 变强了，但我没有》。
这支影片的视觉灵感来自《SHOGUN》，除了使用 GENESIS 胶片模拟插件辅助调色，我还在大量镜头中用到了 Lens Node 插件，以模拟强烈的旋焦和模糊感。而 Lens Node 是个极吃性能的模拟插件，无论 M3 Max 还是 RTX5080，我手里还没有可以在开启它的时候能做到满帧预览的设备。所以，连续渲染几十个挂载了 Lens Node 的镜头，对市面上的绝大部分电脑来说都算是「地狱难度」。
M6 在离电状态下用了 59 分钟完成了渲染，M5 则要用到超过 1 小时 16 分钟。这里要再次强调：大内存直接利好视频剪辑。我一直很依赖第三方插件来模拟胶片光晕、颗粒等效果，此类插件恰恰是吃显存大户。
而且，就在撰写本文期间，我刚好带着 M6 版 MacBook 出差搞了个 Mac 新品上手的短片，虽然导出速度嗖嗖快，但挂载一堆节点加插件之后，还是能明显觉察到它的实时回放的速度不及我的 M3 Max 主力机（因为除了大内存，更多的 GPU 核心和视频编码核心也会影响回放速度），但已经明显强过 16GB 的 M5。所以，如果你的使用场景里也涉及重度调色，那大内存务必得选。
选购建议
内存和硬盘大幅涨价打乱了很多人对一部电脑的价值判断和购机思路，甚至让基础款 MacBook Pro 反而多了几分性价比。M6 芯片提供了足够的性能保障，无论修图、视频剪辑还是运行入门级的 AI 大模型，它都能应对。在此基础上，你原本可能需要加在 Pro 级 SoC 上的预算就能省下来，花给内存或者硬盘。虽然事实上内存和硬盘并没有真的变便宜，但⋯⋯钱包和心理，总有一个得先平衡 :)
而且，在我看来，考虑到涨价潮一时半会还不会褪去，「让用户在 SoC 上少花钱」这件事会显得愈发重要。只要 Apple silicon 的能效比持续进步，那基础款 Mac 的竞争力也一定会继续提升。
简单来说，如果你对电脑有一定的性能要求，但不需要极致澎湃，或者此前在用 MacBook Air，却想拥有更靓的萤幕、更猛的音响、更多的接口，那 M6 版 MacBook Pro 就是对的选择。
很难想象，当 M7 款 MacBook Pro 到来的时候，AI 行业会发展到什么程度，Apple 又会怎样规划自己的产品线。至少在这个当下，如果你真的需要一台新电脑，那就及时行乐罢。

A new bill could change how the US builds energy infrastructure
The midterms are over, and the next big fight could be over permitting reform.
A sweeping bit of legislation designed to cut red tape could speed up the buildout of energy infrastructure in the US.
With the dust barely settled on this week’s momentous midterms, the Senate is set to vote on the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act (BAAJA) on November 9. It’s a plan that could finally start an overhaul of the slow, complicated process for getting energy projects approved, as part of something called permitting reform. People on both sides of the political aisle have been pushing for this type of change for some years.
The key question is whether this will cut greenhouse-gas emissions from the US energy sector or push them higher still.
The federal permitting system is designed to protect the environment against industrial pollution from large projects like power plants, transmission lines, and natural-gas pipelines. Today, permitting is governed by a range of laws, including the National Environmental Policy Act and the Clean Water Act. And if there’s one thing you might know about the current state of permitting in the US it’s that it’s slow and incredibly complicated.
So in its 417 pages, BAAJA attempts to tighten things up. The bill has provisions that narrow the sorts of projects that need to apply for federal permits and others that enact deadlines in hopes of speeding up a process that today can take years. (There are also specific provisions about transmission planning, mining, and the rest of the kitchen sink; this thing is dense.)
One thing to know about this bill is that it’s largely technology neutral: It opens the doors for the buildout of projects involving both fossil fuels and renewables. So there’s a weird mishmash of legislators and outside groups supporting it.
The bill’s four sponsors in the Senate are two Republicans and two Democrats. The White House has backed this permitting reform bill, with its Council of Economic Advisors finding that it would reduce review times and have economic benefits like boosting GDP.
Clean energy groups like the American Clean Power Association have largely come out in favor of the bill too, with the group’s analysis finding that it could unlock 350 gigawatts of new clean energy for the grid.
Some environmental groups, however, oppose the bill, or parts of it. An assessment from the Natural Resources Defense Council says the parts about transmission could have substantial benefits for the grid, but the narrowing of environmental reviews could be damaging for public and private lands.
Many in the climate world have long been calling for permitting reform, arguing that renewables stand to benefit more than fossil fuels. The thinking is that solar and wind power are some of the cheapest sources available today, so if we unlock the ability to build more energy, they are likely to win out economically.
A few early analyses have tried to quantify just what BAAJA would mean for emissions in the US. One, from the think tank Rhodium Group, found that in the best-case scenario, the bill would cut economy-wide emissions by 4%, reduce annual energy bills by $78 per household, and add 200 gigawatts of new clean energy by 2040. In the worst case, emissions would stay about the same.
Another report, from the nonprofit research group Resources for the Future, found that the bill could cut power-sector emissions 6% by 2041 and save more than $350 billion on system costs by midcentury.
But there are some concerns that this permitting bill’s real effect would be to open the floodgates for new natural-gas plants. As data centers have put more pressure on the grid, a wave of such plants have been announced.
“You get down to a trade-off between essentially growing the clean electricity industry on the one hand and then, as a compromise, growing the fossil-fuel industry on the other,” says Kevin Kircher, an energy modeler and assistant professor of mechanical engineering at Purdue.
To quantify that trade-off, Kircher also modeled the potential emissions effects of the bill and found that if BAAJA had been in place in 2025, energy sector emissions would have been higher than the actual baseline. Health and mortality risks associated with air pollution would also have increased.
This bill is scheduled for a Senate vote on November 9, well before the next Congress takes control. It’s not clear what the dramatic victories for Democrats might mean for the legislation, which was expected to pass the Senate with largely Republican support if a few Democrats got on board. Democrats could decide to wait for next year, when they’ll have control of both chambers. But any deal would ultimately need to win Trump’s signature, limiting what a future bill might be able to change.
In a world where permitting new projects is easier, we’ll see more clean energy, but we’ll see more natural gas, too. And while prices could get lower, that could push demand for electricity higher, leading to more emissions overall.
Simplifying the permitting system and cutting red tape is a worthy goal. But when it comes to emissions, it still really matters what, exactly, you want to build.
