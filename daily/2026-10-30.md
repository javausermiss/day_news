Superpowers is a complete software development methodology for your coding agents, built on top of a set of composable skills and some initial instructions that make sure your agent uses them.
It starts from the moment you fire up your coding agent. As soon as it sees that you're building something, it doesn't just jump into trying to write code. Instead, it steps back and asks you what you're really trying to do.
Once it's teased a spec out of the conversation, it shows it to you in chunks short enough to actually read and digest.
After you've signed off on the design, your agent puts together an implementation plan that's clear enough for an enthusiastic junior engineer with poor taste, no judgement, no project context, and an aversion to testing to follow. It emphasizes true red/green TDD, YAGNI (You Aren't Gonna Need It), and DRY.
Next up, once you say "go", it launches a subagent-driven-development process, having agents work through each engineering task, inspecting and reviewing their work, and continuing forward. It's not uncommon for your agent to work autonomously for a couple hours at a time without deviating from the plan you put together.
There's a bunch more to it, but that's the core of the system. And because the skills trigger automatically, you don't need to do anything special. Your coding agent just has Superpowers.

Macro is the all-in-one workspace for you and your team. It unifies email + messages + docs + tasks + agents + CRM into a single fast interface with shared team-level memory. Everything in your workspace is @linked and searchable so your team (and your agents) never have to switch tools.
We built Macro because we wanted a single operating system for our startup. There are many good software products, and we used them all — Slack, Linear, Notion, HubSpot, and Superhuman — but they don't work together as one system. As we scaled our last venture to ~20 people things started to break: every team got their own tools and the company was held together by MCP and Zapier. The company was not computable. It was chaotic.
Macro is a complete redesign of work software from the ground up as a single system.
Designed by us in NYC and Toronto, dogfooded by our team of ~15 for two years. Built in SolidJS and Rust for speed and reliability. We're focused on building something that any small company or team at a larger company can use as their "operating system".
Macro is composed of 'blocks' designed to be modular, extensible, and work together like Lego. For each block, we studied the best prior art and tried to make it even better.
Each surface is purpose-built for its job rather than composed from a generic block primitive — but every one of them shares the same backend; cross-references between a doc and a task, or a channel message and an email, are natively stored as a bidirectional graph.
Macro Mail is inspired by Superhuman's keyboard-first interface with a few key additions:
- Multi-account. Triage all your Google accounts in a single inbox, with the same tagging and sharing system. Or triage individually.
- Unified inbox: emails, messages, @mentions, and tasks to complete, all in the same list. Use j k and e to navigate everything.
- Better AI, with a tools/MCP surface designed to work across inboxes and to help your agents more accurately retrieve information. For example, we expose a unified search tool that allows agents to search all file attachment PDFs (parsed out of email) directly, rather than pulling email threads then attachments. You can also draft, edit and send emails right from AI chats, without opening your email.
- Multitasking ability — Macro has a built-in window manager that lets you create 3+ splits (scales with monitor size) so you can draft emails while reviewing prior threads.
- Company/Contact objects. Macro has native CRM capability so you can cmd+k to a contact, like tim@acme.com to see all emails between you and that person, or companies, to see all emails and files between everyone on your team and everyone at that company, e.g. @acme.com. All of this right from your email without having to open a heavyweight CRM like HubSpot or Salesforce. Email aggregation by contact or company is also available to your agents so they can better assist with CRM-type queries and actions.
Macro Mail lives in the same interface as channels, docs, tasks, and code. From any email, hit "task" to create a linked task, e.g. a ticket for an engineer from a customer support email. @mention emails in documents, e.g. @Re: Contract Signature.eml inside of Todos.md. In Macro, your email is brought into the fold with all of your tools, and your team, in the same permissions system: just hit Share to share an email to any DM or channel — no need to screenshot.
Macro Chat is designed to be more focused than Slack. The first couple of replies show inline and the rest collapse into a thread, so a busy channel stays readable. Threads are permissioned severally so you can share threads across channels by copying links. Everything is stored in a bidirectional graph, so tasks @link to messages that created them, customer support emails tie into support channels, CRM records get updated when they're discussed in messages, etc. The core idea is that (i) messaging should be the centerpiece around which tasks, mail, docs, and content management are built, all in a lightweight way, and agents should be first-class citizens like human users and (ii) messaging needs to be more focused and readable for technical conversations, and not turn into battles where context is lost and progress is indistinguishable from noise.
Linear recently published a report that issue tracking is dead. We agree with that, but the stronger form is that issue tracking never really worked, at least for us. We really tried and we blamed ourselves, but as we talked to other companies, it turns out that nobody was using their issue tracker "correctly". And if that's the case, the problem is the design of the tool, not the companies that use it.
The core problem with traditional issue trackers or project management tools is that they get out of date. The reason they get out of date is that (i) they're a separate system from where the conversation really happens in team chat (e.g. Slack, Macro, Discord, etc.) and (ii) they don't add much benefit beyond tracking the work. They're a chore with near-term costs and only the promise of long-term benefit. They're too rigid compared to a 2D canvas, too opinionated, and don't match how your project actually functions.
The solution isn't to forgo tracking entirely. We tried that and it was a different form of chaos. The solution we've found is lightweight issues tightly coupled to your channels and DMs, so that issue tracking naturally occurs where the conversation itself happens.

Sylphide OS
What is it ?
Sylphide OS is a general-purpose Operating system for computers made from microcontrollers (MCU).
Lacking MMU, those boards are usually not able to run a full-fledge modern operating system such as Linux.
Yet they are now powerful enough to provide an environment very similar to home computers as we knew them from the 80's.
Context
Retrocomputing is all the rage nowadays, but it can mean a lot of different things.
There are people "restoring old computers" (mostly for playing and collecting), typically telling you that emulators dont feel genuine enough for their old memories.
Others, die-hard electronic freaks, are interested in 'recreation' of old computers, either with modern components (cheap, easy to source), or on the contrary with era-exact chips for those who can afford them.
There are emulators of all kind, at different layers (pure-software, FPGA based, cycle-accurate or higher level, ...). You can even find online emulators for almost all platforms before 1990.
Some are developing software, usually games, using modern computers and software, but targeting old computers.
This project is about yet another way: computers using modern microcontollers. There are cheap and more and more common. And of course we expect more will be available.
Purpose
Traditionally, computers are based on powerful processors, with lot of peripherals, more importantly keyboard, screen and mouse, while microcontollers are a lot less powerful, packing a small core with very little RAM and lot of embedded style peripherals on the same chip. Things like UART, SPI, ADC, I2C, counters, timers, and so on.
But of course, as technology evolves, the difference got blurred, and we start to see microcontrollers that can handle "computer-like" system design. It's not so much about compute power (MCUs have been very powerful from some time already), but rather about being able to output to a standard display like VGA or HDMI, rather than nothing, SPI-based small LCD screens, or DSI. To a lesser extent, input can also be challenging, lot of MCUs just have no USB host capability, and when they do, only one port can be used. You want at least two for keyboard and mouse, and ideally a third one for usb key.
When you manage to get all of that, you still have a severely constrained environment (low ram, low colors if any). It really feels like early computers from 80's/90's, both as a user and as a programmer. This is exactly the kind of 'retrocomputing' I'm after.
Here, a "computer" is defined as having:
- keyboard and mouse input, usually either ps/2 or USB
- a standard display connector: VGA or HDMI
- a real framebuffer: no 'on-the-fly' video signal generation
- storage, usually microSD card, but USB keys can also do
Network (ethernet/wifi) is not required, but is a great feature to have.
As a summary, it's targeted to platforms between "development boards" (MCU without display/keyboard/mouse) and Single-board computer which are usually able to run Linux.
This is open hardware, the PCB is designed with KiCad, and source files for both software and hardware can be found on the (mercurial) repository at https://freehackers.org/electronics/sylphide-os.
It is developed by Thomas Capricelli.
It's called Sylphide OS because Sylphide Consulting is the name under which I work as a consultant.
Hardware
From the beginning, Sylphide OS was designed to run on different platforms. Here are the currently targets, each one has a dedicated page.
- Olimex esp32-p4-pc, the flagship product: new, powerful
- Barebone raspberry pi Pico 2, my own minimalist attempt at VGA+USB on a rp2350.
- FabGL, an old classic with PS/2 input and VGA
- There's an emulator that runs on the (linux) host, used for development.
I got some other targets in mind. If you know of readily hardware that could use such an OS, tell me about it. Or even better, start a port by yourself (but please contact me first).
Software
Nowadays, a PC operating system is a big bundle of different software (kernel, drivers, libraries, programs). But "back in the days", it was rather an integrated and self contained program, focused on the graphical interface. Drivers were few, because each computer manufacturer, or even each computer model had it's own dedicated operating system.
That's what I want to recreate here.
Under the hood, Sylphide OS uses FreeRTOS, a low-level kernel typically used on this kind of platform. It provides helpers for running tasks and some low-level routines.
On top of that, Sylphide OS adds:
- drivers for mouse, keyboard, display, storage and network
- graphical interface
- programs
It aims to be versatile enough to be usable on very limited platforms (small framebuffer, monochrome display, tiny RAM), while still being able to use features for higher specs ones.
The code is mostly C (low-level, drivers, rendering library) and C++ (for the graphical interface). Some targets use assembler for drivers. C++ is used in a very limited form, using only features that would not bloat software. You really want to be very careful with such limited resources.
Current status
Keep in minde it's only 0.1.
The focus was on having a clean/stable framework and proper drivers for a few targets.
What you get:
- kconfig-based configuration
- Framework: different SDKs, different platforms, tooling
- Low-level rendering: framebuffer, bitmaps, fonts
- Three targets (+ emulator), with proper drivers
- Graphical interface with desktop, menu, windows, windowmanager, desktop icons. You might recognize a famous look.
- Information panel displaying hardware and software details and demonstrating the color capabilities.
What you don't have yet, but definitely planned:
- storage (microsd, usb)
- network (ethernet, wifi)
- widgets (button, checkbox, modal dialog, ..)
- external programs (loading an executable from sdcard and execute it)

I got interested in looped transformers, where one transformer block is applied several times with the same weights instead of stacking different layers. Reusing weights is cheap on memory, so I wanted to see if a looped world model could be small enough to run on a microcontroller. I picked the Pico 2 W because it is cheap and has an FPU, and small games because I could write the real versions directly into numpy and check every generated frame against them. Auto-dismisses the macOS prompt:
Allow "Google Chrome" to find devices on local networks? This will allow the app to discover, connect to, and collect data from devices on your networks.
It presses Don't Allow for you, within a fraction of a second, every time. Zero CPU while idle. Survives reboots. Two short Swift files, no dependencies.
macOS (Sequoia and later) shows this prompt the first time an app touches the local network. The decision is supposed to be remembered. For many apps it isn't, so the prompt comes back on every launch, often for apps you aren't even using:
- Chromium browsers (Chrome, Brave, Edge, Island, Arc) copy their own app bundle into a random temp folder at launch so the code signature survives in-place updates. macOS keys the Local Network decision to that random path, so the next launch looks like a brand-new app. Your Privacy settings fill up with duplicate "Google Chrome.app" rows that no longer point at anything.
- Apps with versioned install paths (anything that installs into .../versions/1.2.3/) get a new row per update.
- Misattributed prompts. macOS pins the dialog on whichever app it decides is "responsible", so you'll see prompts for apps that aren't running.
- Some apps do legitimately use this permission: Chromecast and AirPlay discovery in browsers and media players, LAN multiplayer in games, printer and scanner utilities. If you use one of those features, answer Allow for that app (dontallow pause 10m while you do), and it's remembered as long as the app's path is stable. If you never use them, you never need to be asked.
There is no supported way to prune the list, no MDM payload to pre-deny the permission, and tccutil reset doesn't cover it. So: a small agent that answers "no" for you.
brew install johncassil/tap/dontallow   # or: make && make install
dontallow install
dontallow install writes a per-user launchd agent, starts it, opens System Settings > Privacy & Security > Accessibility, and prints the exact path to add if macOS doesn't prompt you. Accessibility is the permission that lets it press the button. It waits for the grant and confirms when the agent is active. dontallow status repeats the instructions any time the grant is missing.
Each dismissal posts a notification naming the app and the pause command, so you never have to remember how to turn it off. Mute the notification in System Settings if you'd rather not see it.
Three options, pick whichever fits:
- dontallow pause 10m, then launch the app and click Allow yourself.
- Add the app's name to ~/.config/dontallow/allowlist (one per line, case-insensitive substring). Prompts for it are never touched.
- Flip the toggle in System Settings > Privacy & Security > Local Network. A dismissed prompt records a deny, which you can reverse there.
Set ~/.config/dontallow/grace to a number of seconds if you want a window to click Allow before the agent does (default 0.25).
The prompt is a window owned by the system process UserNotificationCenter. dontallow registers an Accessibility observer for window creation on that process and sleeps until one fires. It reads the dialog text, confirms it's a local network prompt and not something else, checks pause and allowlist, and performs the Accessibility press action on the Don't Allow button. If the press is refused it falls back to a synthetic click at the button's location.
It never modifies system files, never disables protections, and never chooses Allow.
Local Network cannot be pre-set by MDM, but Accessibility can. For a fleet: build once, sign with a Developer ID, ship the binary plus a PPPC (Privacy Preferences Policy Control) profile granting Accessibility to its code requirement, and run dontallow install per user. No manual Settings step.
macOS keys the Accessibility grant to the binary's code signature, and every build (including brew upgrade) produces a new one. After an upgrade run dontallow status. If it says the grant is missing, remove the dontallow row in Accessibility settings and add it back from the path it prints. The agent re-attaches on its own within ten seconds. A future release signed with a Developer ID will remove this step.

提前拿到了 HomePod mini 2，简单说一下体验。
相比于上一代，时隔六年更新的 HomePod mini 2 在参数方面的变化不算小：芯片从 S5 跃升至 S10，国行版本的 Wi-Fi 连接规格从前一代的 802.11n (Wi-Fi 4) 升级至 802.11ax，也就是 Wi-Fi 6，蓝牙升级到了 Bluetooth 6。
但在体验方面，HomePod mini 2 的提升其实不算明显。从最核心的音频串流能力来说，即使是按照未压缩的 24-bit / 48 KHz 双声道音频来计算，也仅需要约几 Mbps 的吞吐能力，实际使用 ALAC 编码之后的需求通常会更低。因此这一代 HomePod mini 2 在无线连接规格方面的升级，我认为可以算是一次常规的硬件迭代，对日常体验而言不会产生明显影响。
根据官网给出的信息来看，HomePod mini 2 采用的依然是全频驱动单元 + 双振动抵消无源辐射器，内部扬声器架构应该没有变化，关乎于计算音频、语音处理的 S10 芯片就成了影响听感的重要因素。从我的实际听感来说，我认为两代 HomePod mini 的差距极其接近，HomePod mini 2 的低音下探会更足，人声的包裹感会略好一些，日常听歌几乎不会感受到区别，比较可惜的一点是这一代 HomePod mini 与上一代一样仍旧不支持空间音频。

最后说一下购买建议，可以看出，HomePod mini 2 在日常体验方面并没有太多新东西，如果已经在使用 HomePod mini 1 或者第二代 HomePod，其实没有必要急着换新。当然，作为 Apple 门槛相对较低、体验足够完整的智能家居中枢和音箱产品，如果主要需求是控制智能家居，或是在卧室、书房等空间增加一两只体积相对小巧的音箱，HomePod mini 2 毫无疑问是目前更合理的选择。

Matrix 首页推荐
Matrix 是少数派的写作社区，我们主张分享真实的产品体验，有实用价值的经验与思考。我们会不定期挑选 Matrix 最优质的文章，展示来自用户的最真实的体验和观点。
文章代表作者个人观点，少数派仅对标题和排版略作修改。
想象一下，你是一名职业足球运动员，在数以万计的目光注视下，站在十二码罚球线上。这一球很有可能将决定一个重要的冠军归属。对方的门将在门线上做出各种干扰动作，极力试图干扰你的决策、跑动和出球。无论对任何人来说，这无疑都是一个压力（pressure）极大的时刻。
你将选择怎样的策略、指导接下来的这脚射门呢？
- 忽视门将的动作，瞄准某一个固定的角度，大力抽射。
- 仔细观察门将的反应，尝试推射甚至巧射。
如果你是教练，你会怎样指导运动员呢？你会在战术布置环节告诉 TA「别想多，照着那个地方踢」，还是要求 TA「别松懈，仔细观察每一个细节」？
没有哪一种策略是必胜的。在第一种情况下，有人助跑、摆腿一气呵成，皮球如出膛炮弹直挂死角。也有人过分追求角度，一脚将球直接踢上看台。
在第二种情况下，有人敏锐捕捉到门将重心的微小移动，从容地骗过门将、进球得分。也有人机关算尽，最后踢出的球却软弱无力，被门将扑个正着。
「最优策略」离不开「任务」和「人」两个要素
同样的策略选择，有人顶着压力一锤定音，有人直接崩盘葬送比赛。如果把视角从十二码射点球延伸到足球运动的其他技术动作、其他运动项目，甚至于军警技战术和个人防卫，我们会看到许多类似的、有关策略选择和执行的争议。
单纯给策略贴上「简单就是好（别多想）」或「越全面/复杂/精细越好（精算致胜）」的标签是毫无意义的。真正决定成败的，是（1）任务环境本身究竟需要多复杂的信息加工，以及（2）运动员本人在高压当下是否具备足够的认知容量和执行稳定性，以支撑其选择的策略。
也即，问题不在于哪一种策略更优，而在于「谁，面对怎样的任务，选择哪一种策略，更有利于取得结果」。
德国科隆体育大学的 Laura Voigt 博士等人，在《认知科学趋势》（Trends in Cognitive Sciences）最新发表的理论文章，正是为了探讨这样一个核心命题。合著者 Markus Raab 教授是科隆体育大学运动心理系的主任，另外两位合著者 Arne Nieuwenhuys、Rich S.W. Masters 也分别是「压力导致的发挥失常（choking under pressure）」领域的大牛。
在这篇题为《超越注意力：表现压力改变信息加工复杂度（Beyond attention: performance pressure shifts information-processing complexity）》的论文中，作者们跳出了传统理论的争议，提出了一套全新的整合框架：
压力真正改变的，不是抽象的「心态」，也不是具体动作的「发挥」，而是运动员的信息加工复杂度水平。
当运动员的信息加工复杂度水平趋近任务所需的复杂度时，运动员就会顶住压力、「超常」发挥；反之，若信息加工复杂度水平背离任务所需的复杂度，运动员就会表现出发挥失常。
理论框架：当动作控制遇上「偏差-方差权衡」
为了精确刻画这一机制，作者引入了三个环环相扣的核心概念：
第一个概念是信息加工规则与复杂度（Information-processing rules & complexity）。任何高水平的运动表现，都离不开神经系统的信息加工过程。大脑选用什么信息、赋予多大权重，如何整合这些信息，以指导动作选择与执行，上述过程都依赖于具体运动员习得的「规则」。不同的运动员会在自身的实践和教练指导过程中，习得复杂度高低不一的规则。
规则的复杂度越高，调用的信息量和参考的条件越多，计算步骤也越复杂。
以大家熟悉的武侠小说为例。金庸笔下的泰山派有一招「岱宗如何」，本质上要求使用者化身人肉计算机，算准一切条件，一招制敌。这就是复杂规则。
与之相反，古龙笔下的飞剑客从不计算，只是一剑刺穿对手的咽喉，唯快不破。这就是简单规则。
第二个概念来自统计学和决策科学，称作偏差-方差权衡（Bias-Variance Trade-off）。与丹尼尔.卡内曼在《噪声》一书中提出的公式类似，作者将动作控制中出现的误差拆解为三部分：
偏差（bias）的平方 + 方差（variance）+ 不可消除误差（irreducible error）
偏差（Bias）意味着系统性误差。高偏差意味着规则过于简单，或执行者倾向于忽略关键信息。例如，有些球员永远盲目踢向一个习惯的角落，而无视门将动向，这就是高偏差、低方差。这样的球员，射门动作可能很一致，不容易出现临场失误，但容易被对手提前研究而抓到。 原作者专门强调，偏差之所以要平方，正是为了与方差的量纲匹配，并消除方向性。
方差（Variance）则代表执行的不稳定性。高方差意味着规则过于复杂，容易受到外部噪声和线索的影响。例如，有些球员会同时计算门将站位、移动和过往习惯，观察门将的各种动作细节。这样做虽然有助于减少了信息遗漏（低偏差），但计算量可能超出人脑的能力范围，而且容易受到临场干扰。这就是高方差、低偏差。这样的球员，可能时有灵光一现的超神发挥，但在另一些时候也动辄超鬼、令人大跌眼镜。
由此便引出了全篇最核心的第三个概念——任务匹配复杂度（Task-matched complexity）。在以复杂度为横轴、误差为纵轴的坐标系中，随着复杂度提升，偏差曲线下降而方差曲线上升，两者叠加形成的总误差呈一条 U 型曲线。位于该曲线谷底的「黄金甜区」代表着任务匹配复杂度，也即与当前运动任务最适配的复杂度——不多不少，刚刚好。
作者认为，这个甜区并非一成不变的常数，而是由两个因素共同决定的动态平衡点。也即，任务本身的客观需求，以及运动员个体的因素（如技能熟练度和工作记忆容量）。
两种选择，四种加工结果
前述的两种策略选择，各自对应着两个截然不同的认知调控方向。每种方向又存在着「恰当」与「过度」，或者说「方向合理」「方向不合理」的分野，进而很大程度上决定了动作执行的结果。
压力之下，大脑究竟会「向左走（简化）」还是「向右走（复杂化）」？理论指出，这取决于压力诱发的、具体球员内心的具体心理反应。
在压力情境下，有些运动员想到了奖金、成绩或社会评价，动员了自身的努力（effort mobilization），提升了动机水平，于是更容易选择复杂化。反之，另一些运动员产生了更多的焦虑与担忧，注意力转向「受威胁」的主观体验，就会更容易选择简化。此外，运动员如果能主观感知到自身算力吃紧，也会主动选择战略性简化（strategic simplification）作为补偿策略。
在没有压力的情境下，不同的运动员的信息加工风格是不同的。有人平时倾向于「想多」「精算」，基线复杂度高于理论上的甜区。有人一贯喜欢「凭感觉」「莽一脚」，基线复杂度低于甜区。这个基线水平，叠加压力下个人的感受，以及随之而来的简化或复杂化选择，就会产生四种不同的结果。
平时容易想太多的运动员，在压力情境下主动降低复杂度，大力抽射预设死角，就是合理的简化策略（Simplification）：放弃对助跑和摆腿动作的分步拆解控制，也不去理会门将充满干扰的假动作，而是将射门动作打包成一个高度自动化的「高阶动作组块（higher-order movement chunk）」，避免高压情境下的大脑过载。
如果这种简化发生在平时就比较看感觉的运动员身上，就会变成过度简化（Oversimplification）：完全忽视守门员的动作、不考虑对方对自身技术特点的研究，恰好落入对方的预判；或者，不留意临场条件、自身体能状态，在头脑中过度执着于死角和力量，将球直接踢飞。
与之相对，平时比较随性的运动员，在压力情境下主动提升复杂度，选择冷静观察门将再推射，就是合理的复杂化策略（Complexification）。
很显然，不是每个球员（或者每个球员在所有比赛场合）都能胜任这样的复杂信息加工。如果平时就容易想多，或者虽然「努力多想」但大脑已经不堪重负，就会在「既要又要还要」的过度分析中掉进过度复杂化（Overcomplexification）的大坑。这会导致射门动作本身趋于破碎，踢出了半高球或绵软的贴地球，送给对方一个大礼。
两种理论也许都对，只是分人、分场合
上述新理论框架的提出，有助于解决 choking under pressure 研究领域的两个难题。
运动心理学界关于发挥失常的理论主要分为两大阵营。合著者 Masters 本人是「自我关注/再投资理论（Self-focus / Reinvestment）」的提出者。这一派理论基于动作学习「认知-联结-自动化」的三阶段理论，认为压力可能会、也可能不会提升焦虑水平。无论是否焦虑，只要当表现显得十分重要，或与预期结果存在差距时，运动员即可能启动自我关注，投入额外努力，对本来自动化的动作进行逐步的、过度的监控，进而导致自动化破坏（de-automatization）和动作崩解。
另一位合著者 Nieuwenhuys 则基于分心理论（Distraction accounts）/注意控制理论（Attention Control Theory, ACT）进行了大量研究。这一派理论认为压力会提升焦虑水平，继而关注外在的威胁性刺激、忽略与任务相关的信息，从而导致动作控制和执行的退化。
这两种看似相反的理论各自得到了一些实证研究结果的支持，令学界莫衷一是。本文可以看做两派研究者试图达成共识的一次尝试。自我关注与分心，也许并不是非此即彼的竞争关系。人在压力下可以努力动员，也可以焦虑；可以试图简化，也可以继续复杂化。两种反应在同一个复杂度光谱上左右移动，再以具体任务性质决定的甜区作为基准，就会产生四种反应：
向右的运动如果靠近甜区，就是「合理复杂化」；如果远离甜区，则是「过度复杂化」或者所谓的「再投资」。
分心或受到干扰对应着向左运动，同样分为远离甜区的「过度简化」和靠近甜区的「合理简化/战略性简化」。
这种四分的结果，还可以解释另外一个实践中常常出现的问题：同样都是压力，为什么有的运动员在有些时候掉链子（choking），另一些运动员在另一些时候会超常发挥（clutch performance）？新理论敏锐地指出，复杂度偏移的方向，与最终表现的结果，是可以相互独立的。也即，单纯讨论「别想多」或者「多想点」是不够的，必须要纳入人的要素，并且放在具体的情境中进行分析。
启示：从实验室建模，到赛场抗压训练
本文第一作者 Laura Voigt 博士，是我在德国科隆体育大学的硕士论文导师。我的论文是 Voigt 博士研究项目中的一个子课题，其主要目的便是汇总分析「压力对运动表现之影响」的实验结果。
有趣的是，我的初步分析结果似乎表明，能够左右压力对运动表现影响的要素，并不是运动任务、运动员的经验或具体的压力源，而是「实验由谁主持」以及「实验在哪个国家/地区/文化圈」进行。
这个结果与新理论的方向是一致的：同样的压力要素和实验范式，由不同的研究者作用于不同文化背景的受试者，可能引发 choking，可能不起作用，也可能导致 clutch。未来的压力操控研究在设计时，或许必须纳入以下三个考量：
- 具体的任务，其任务匹配信息加工复杂度，处于什么水平？也即，任务本身是相对简单、无对抗和博弈的（如篮球罚球、飞镖投掷、奥运会射击、高尔夫推杆）还是相对复杂、存在对抗和博弈的（如射点球、军警战术射击、电子竞技）？
- 具体的受试者，其信息加工风格和规则复杂度，是高于还是低于任务的复杂度水平？
- 具体的压力源会让受试者的复杂度偏好如何偏移？也即，是会更多地产生努力动员（趋向于复杂化），还是感受到威胁（趋向于简化）？
作者在原文结尾还给出了清晰的可证伪边界：如果压力稳定改变了运动表现，却测不到与之独立的复杂度变化，或者，当运动员的复杂度水平靠近预期的任务匹配复杂度、但却导致了运动表现水平的降低，则会证伪该理论。
即使先不考虑理论本身的真伪，这个新理论的提出，至少为教练员和运动员提供了一个简单而深刻的启示：运动心理学的分析、思考和干预切忌一刀切，必须具体问题具体分析。
许多教练，特别是基层教练，特别容易凭借自身的经验、采用单一处方治疗百病。例如，我见过的某位格斗教练，对所有新学员都会喊「别多想、凭本能打（盲目简化）」；另一位网球教练，则喜欢自己带头想多（盲目复杂化）。二者都是不可取的。
「抗压训练」的核心可能首先在于个性化诊断与精准匹配：教练必须摸清队员平时的习惯起点，对压力的反应倾向，以及高压下的工作记忆容量。对于平时大老粗、靠感觉、倾向于忽略和简化信息的运动员，教练应当激发其努力程度，提高其工作记忆和信息加工水平，训练这些运动员刻意的复杂化能力。
反之，对于平时心思重、想法多、倾向于追求细节的运动员，教练则应当给其设计高可靠性、低复杂度的「战略简化」预案——例如，直接告诉 TA往哪边踢。
再回到开篇令人窒息的点球大战，或者其他任何一个运动项目的决战赛场，你是否能够稍微放下对「大心脏」或「必胜策略」的执念，开始思考如何将二者匹配起来？踢出高射炮的无奈，或者软弱推射的遗憾，也许既不代表「这样踢肯定进不了」，也不代表「这个人天生就不是赢家」——一脚饮恨的结果，也许只是因为压力将具体的球员推离了平衡点，要么偏差爆表，要么方差失控。
一切对抗运动都免不了心理上的博弈。即使身处台风眼的附近，依然能够认清自己，明确任务，稳稳地踩中「任务匹配复杂度」的甜区。所谓定力，所谓赢家，大抵就是如此。

在上一篇文章《让 Agent 读懂你的身体：数据获取篇》中，我花了很大的篇幅讲清楚了第一个问题：Agent 怎样拿到数据。如果照着实践完，你大概率已经收获了一份存储了运动健康数据的 SQLite、Markdown 或 JSON 文件。
此时，大多数人大概就忍不住让 AI Agent 来帮忙分析一下文件了——
「Claude，这是我的健康运动数据，帮我做一下详细的分析。」
我自己起初也是这么做的。在简短的提问之后，Claude 就能生成一个精美的 HTML。上面有每个动作的训练趋势、恢复状态的图标以及按照日历排列的训练日历图等。信息排得很满，但研究一下就会发现，它除了好看，并没有什么用。
这就是问题所在——AI Agent 擅长描述，而如果想让它分析实际问题并给出建议，比如下一次训练该加多少重量，则需要用户自己对需求的判断与适当引导。
所以，在这篇文章中，我会根据我的实际经验，来详细回答后面的两个问题：拿到数据以后，怎样让它正确地处理？它给出的结论能信几分？
注：目前我这套方法只适用于辅助日常的运动训练，不建议用于排查身体的实际症状，也不应用于应对医疗情况。
怎样让 Agent 正确处理数据
我的整体方案
以我自己为例，我用：
- Apple Watch 记录运动训练数据，包括有氧和抗阻训练；
- Oura Ring 记录睡眠、夜间恢复以及日常活动数据；
- 训记 App 专门记录抗阻训练的容量和组数（同时使用训记和 Apple Watch 不会重复计入运动数据）
其中，Apple Health 和 Oura 的数据都接进第三方服务 Freddy。它通过 Oura 的官方接口授权读取，所以 Oura 自己算出的准备指数（Readiness）等算法数据也能拿到。
接着，通过 Agent 预先写好的 Python 脚本，经 Freddy 和训记的 API 将数据拉取到本地，存进 SQLite 数据库。而体脂、体重这类低频指标和训练感受等，我会用文字或图片的形式直接发给 Agent，由它整理后录入数据库。
上述项目搭建在家里的 NAS 上，Python 脚本定时拉取最新的生理和训练数据；网页看板设置好端口，通过局域网地址就可以直接访问。
去健身房之前，我用手机或电脑通过局域网连上 NAS 触发任务。常规任务在网页看板上留有按钮，可以一键启动；复杂的任务则通过终端里的 CLI 工具对话进行。触发后，AI Agent 读取最新的身体数据和训练记录，先由两个「教练」给出动作、组数和重量建议，再由「循证教练」核查训练量和强度。最终由我自己确认后，将计划作为一条训练记录，通过 API 写进训记。
到了健身房，我只要打开训记照着计划练，并给每组动作备注余量和训练感受。这些内容最终会通过 API 回传给 AI，完成一次循环。
把这些环节串起来并不复杂。真正需要花心思的，是给 Agent 建立一套「行为规范」，让它稳定地分析已有的数据并输出建议。方法很简单，搞个 Skill 就行。
为什么不用现成 Skill
「不要重复发明轮子」是软件开发领域的至理名言。一般来说，我们应该先看看社区里已有的 Skill，如果它们可以覆盖需求，直接套用即可。
其中，Coach Paddy 相对最接近我个人的需求。它内置了数据接口，能够读取 Garmin、COROS、WHOOP 和 Apple Watch 的数据，力量训练数据可以选配训记的 API；记忆存在用户的 Obsidian 里，包括运动员档案、训练计划和每日生理数据等。它会给出准备度评分，用来决定当天全力、降级还是休息，也能提供训练计划、进行每周复盘，算是一套相对完整的解决方案。
Coach Paddy 还有个值得借鉴的机制：出现数据缺失时，准备度评分就会降级，替换为 1 到 5 分的主观精力评分。
我最终没有选择 Coach Paddy。首先，我不需要它的数据接口模块。而且它虽然自称支持多项运动，分析的重心却在耐力训练和赛事备战，有比赛信心分、比赛倒计时等功能，和我的应用场景不吻合。此外，项目的 Star 和 Fork 不多，说明实际效果可能验证不足。
其实，现有的 Skill 多多少少都有类似的问题。因为训练是一件很个人的事情：训练目标、关注的指标、可用的器械、作息习惯、运动体系等，人人都不一样，现成的 Skill 很难完美适配。同时，健康和训练类 Skill 的关注度普遍较低，我目前找到的最多也不过 200 多个 Star，使用反馈大都不足。

- 统计时间为 2026.10.16；
- Stars 一栏中的「-」表示该 Skill 为项目的子文档，没有单独的 Star；
- 类型一栏是我的主观分类，仅供参考。
所以，如果你能接触到顶级模型（例如 Claude Fable 或 ChatGPT Astra 等），不如让 Agent 吸取现成 Skill 的可取之处，在此之上定制一个适配个人习惯的 Skill。
定制 Skill，最难也是最关键的一步就是把需求梳理清楚，然后制定详细的实施方案。我会先用 grill-me 技能把需求问清楚。它来自 Matt Pocock 的开源 Skill 仓库，作用是让 Agent 反过来向你提问：从粗略的想法出发，一直追问到形成确定而详细的结论。坏处就是有时候你会觉得，它怎么还有这么多问题。

备份数据
发现误登录以后，第一件事就是立即断开设备的网络，随后要做好数据无法完整恢复的心理准备。因为 iOS 设备备份必定包含 Apple 账户信息，登入了陌生账户后的备份是无法完整恢复的。并且如果设备持续联网，一旦对方开启丢失模式或远程抹掉设备，尚未备份的数据就完全无法找回了。
因此，只要设备仍能正常解锁，在断网的情况下尽快将数据进行本地备份。建议创建加密备份，尽可能地保留健康数据、密码等内容。备份的方法这里不再赘述，通过 Mac 的 Finder、Windows 的 Apple 设备、其他第三方软件，都可以进行本地备份。
提交移除申请
发起申请
Apple 提供了一个专属的支持入口，在该页中的「需要更多支持」中可以发起激活锁支持请求。
阅读使用条款并同意后，点击「继续」。
继续填入相关信息。对于电子邮件地址，建议此前在设备上登入了 iCloud 的 Apple 账户。序列号则照常填入，但务必确认与后续提交佐证材料中的序列号一致。

We urgently need to monitor AI agents’ actions in real time
Right now, we’re effectively flying blind when deploying these tools. Here’s what we need to do instead.
This summer, hundreds of OpenAI agents tried to cheat on a cybersecurity test. They broke out of their test environment and successfully hacked Hugging Face, a site that they believed held clues about how they’d be graded. Around the same time, another OpenAI agent accessed private files from an Australian government website. It’s a problem that’s by no means confined to OpenAI. In the months since, Anthropic, Google, and Meta have also disclosed incidents in which their agents have gone off track. And worryingly, in many cases the agents eluded the very researchers and safety teams tasked with monitoring them.
AI agents are taking unauthorized actions, and the companies building and running them are mostly relying on luck and hindsight to mitigate any harmful impacts. They must do more to ensure safety. That’s why EU regulators and US agencies are rightfully calling for agent developers and deployers to monitor agents in real time. But these rules assume that companies can see what their agents are doing and why—when in reality, they lack sufficient visibility.
As agents roll out across critical sectors, from finance to government to medicine, we must be able to monitor them in real time to prevent wide-ranging and potentially devastating consequences. And while safety depends on monitoring, monitoring requires observability: the capacity to capture what the system did, process it into signals, and present those to humans or automated oversight systems. In other words, we need to know what the agent did and why it did it. And as our recent research shows, current agent frameworks—the software that runs agents— do not reliably generate the crucial observability records we need to ensure that agents are deployed safely.
Agents present new kinds of problems that generative AI alone does not. They inherit the unpredictability of the LLMs they are built on, but where a generative AI system produces a single output for a human to act on, an agent performs longer tasks—it plans, picks tools, spawns sub-agents, and takes actions. So an agent’s course can take shape in a multitude of different ways, and no tests before launch can fully anticipate the risks. This makes observing the agents while they work even more challenging, and even more important.
With colleagues from academia, civil society, and industry, we studied the four most widely used agent frameworks to see what signals they actually emit. We found major gaps that made it hard to achieve key safety goals, like noticing policy violations, unauthorized actions, and even human oversight failures. Key observability requirements were missing—like Agent IDs that persist over time, and records about which humans approved agent tasks. This made it hard to tell which agents did what or assess whether they were taking unauthorized actions.
The good news is that these gaps, once identified, can be closed. Doing so starts with capturing two types of records: those that reveal what happened in an agent’s run and how the agent made its decisions. The “what happened” records identify the agent (while preserving privacy) and log how humans intervened and whether agent permissions or “memory” shifted, while the “decision” records show an agent’s reasoning and levels of confidence in the actions it’s taken.
Take, for example, agents working to process a bank’s mortgage loans. The system examines an application and recommends denying it, and then a human loan officer signs off on the decision. In a later audit, or perhaps even in real time, a bank or regulator needs to be able to reconstruct which agent made the call, which officer approved it, and how long they took. And the bank must also analyze records of how the agent arrived at its refusal decision in order to ensure it did not use off-limits information, like details about the neighborhood the applicant is from.
Recording “what happened” and “how the agent decided” require different approaches, but both are feasible. The current lack of “what happened” records is mostly a software plumbing problem that can be addressed through OpenTelemetry, an open observability standard maintained by engineers across the industry and by the agent frameworks adopting it.
OpenTelemetry has launched a working group drafting shared formats for tracing agents, but they have yet to be finalized. The group should urgently incorporate standards for maintaining persistent agent identity and for recording human interventions and changes to agents’ permission or memory. And it should be done before these systems can scale, all the while attending to the privacy implications of agent ID that links to real people.
Preserving “how agents decided” records is more challenging. The issue raises open research questions for model providers and researchers: Does the step-by-step reasoning a model displays, known as chain of thought, reflect how the model actually made a decision? And do we have reliable ways to measure how confident the agent is in that decision? These questions need answers before anyone sets standards for how such observability records are used.
No single organization or sector can close the observability gaps on their own, but together, they can support the infrastructure required to prevent dangerous incidents and support safety.
AI developers building agent frameworks, like OpenAI, Anthropic, CrewAI, and LangChain, must integrate telemetry signals recording an agent’s persistent identity, any human interventions, and any changes to an agent’s permissions and memory. Meanwhile, enterprises adopting these agent frameworks have an influential role to play; enterprises in regulated industries, like banks and hospitals, can put pressure on the agent developers to supply these missing observability records where standards do not yet exist.

Our climate models have a big, dangerous blind spot
A powerful feedback loop could drive more warming than predicted.
Climate change itself is increasingly fueling climate change. Rising concentrations of greenhouse gases are driving up global temperatures, setting off dangerous feedback loops that we have not fully accounted for in our climate models—or our emissions-reduction goals.
Scientists have long warned that warming would propel further warming in various ways, but a growing number of observations and studies are at last providing a sharper sense of just how powerful these cascading effects could be. And, by extension, they’re showing how much faster the planet may warm in this century.
A study in Environmental Research Letters in September, synthesizing the results of previous papers, found that the additional carbon dioxide and methane released from thawing permafrost, intensifying wildfires, hotter lakes and reservoirs, and wetter, warmer wetlands alone could drive global average temperatures up 0.2 °C to 0.4 °C this century. That would come on top of the 2.6°C of warming above preindustrial levels that modelers are already expecting through 2100. The additional heat could subject many of the world’s cities to monthslong heat waves and expose hundreds of millions of people to flooding.
None of the models that inform the major UN climate reports, which assess how much the planet will likely warm under various emissions scenarios, account for all these feedback effects. Many of them don’t incorporate any. That leaves a huge blind spot in our understanding of how much the world could actually warm in the coming decades—and how rapidly nations will need to slash emissions to stay within critical boundaries.
A separate analysis published in One Earth in March, which examined what the feedback problem means for our international climate goals, concluded that the world may be much further off track than is widely believed. It found that just three regional sources of warming-driven emissions—thawing permafrost, increasing fires in boreal forests, and warming tropical wetlands—may shorten by as much as 25% the time before the planet consistently breaches the 1.5 °C and 2 °C warming thresholds.
Our climate models and the UN’s reports clearly need to be updated to better represent these effects. Nations must also step up their efforts to reduce emissions to reflect this improving understanding of the dangers we face, says Sam Abernethy, the lead author of the paper and a climate scientist at Spark Climate Solutions, a San Francisco–based nonprofit research group.
At least some of this is starting to happen. Spark kick-started an initiative, called the Warming-Induced Greenhouse Gas Emissions Model Intercomparison Project, through which more than 20 international teams are running the same set of analyses on different models to develop greater understanding of feedback effects’ potential power. Researchers involved hope that the UN will incorporate those results into its next major climate assessment, due out in 2029. That report will update estimates of the remaining emissions that can be released before the world crosses key temperature thresholds.
But what is already clear is that the planet is rapidly warming and that nations haven’t done nearly enough to cut fossil-fuel pollution. And even if we did manage to halt it, warming-driven emissions could continue.
So a number of the research groups involved in the recent studies have started to explore what nations could do to get feedback emissions under control. Scientists at the Woodwell Climate Research Center in Massachusetts are, for example, investigating the possibility of preventing fires or rapidly detecting them and putting them out when they threaten to burn the vegetation and soil that help insulate particularly carbon-rich wedges of permafrost (a subject I explored at length in a recent feature story). In addition, the Environmental Defense Fund’s Climate Innovation Initiative has backed lab research on the potential of adding sulfate, iron, or other compounds to wetlands to counteract fast-rising methane emissions.
Several startups are also exploring possibilities. They’re looking into suppressing the lightning strikes that are igniting more and more fires in high latitudes, placing synthetic mats above permafrost to prevent it from melting, and deploying autonomous drones to quickly suppress potentially high-emissions fires before they burn out of control.
Investigating all these ideas is worthwhile. But researchers are only in the earliest stages of figuring out how big a dent in emissions any such efforts could achieve across sprawling global ecosystems undergoing massive transformations.
The surest path to reducing the effect of warming-driven warming remains the one we’ve known all along: breaking society’s addiction to fossil fuels.

            Abstract:I consider what it means for AI assistance to save time. I contrast a stopwatch view, on which time saved is whatever the clock records, with a ledger view, on which it is a counterfactual that has to be read next to the errors the work lets through, and I argue for the latter. The argument has four parts. I define a model-free pair of quantities, time saved and excess escaped defects, and one decision number built from them: the break-even defect cost. I give a baseline model of checking in which, until the model is good enough not to check, a calibrated worker's escaped-defect rate does not depend on how good the model is; checking time grows with the logarithm of the model's error rate; and over-trust buys minutes on the clock at a larger cost in total minutes.
