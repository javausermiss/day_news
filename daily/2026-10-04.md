这些不难实现，但是需要自己折腾配置

Agent Reach 是一个能力层（capability layer），不是又一个工具。
它比任何具体实现高一层——负责选型、安装、体检、路由，不负责底层读取本身。读取由 Agent 直接调用上游工具完成，没有包装层。
你给一个新 Agent 装环境的时候，总要花时间去找工具、装依赖、调配置——Twitter 用什么读？Reddit 怎么登录？小红书的 CLI 停更了换什么？每次都要重新踩一遍。Agent Reach 做的事情很简单：当下最稳的接入方式，我们替你选好、装好、体检好。接入方式会换代（2026 年 3 月一批单平台 CLI 集体停更，我们换了路由），你不用操心。

Agent Reach 在设计上重视安全：
⚠️ 封号风险提醒： 使用 Cookie 登录的平台（Twitter、小红书等），通过脚本/API 调用存在被平台检测并封号的风险。请务必使用专用小号，不要用你的主账号。
需要 Cookie 或登录态的平台（Twitter、小红书、Reddit、Facebook、Instagram 等）建议使用专用小号，不要用主账号。原因有二：
- 封号风险 — 平台可能检测到非正常浏览器的 API 调用行为，导致账号被限制或封禁
- 安全风险 — Cookie 等同于完整登录权限，用小号可以在凭据泄露时限制影响范围

Superpowers is a complete software development methodology for your coding agents, built on top of a set of composable skills and some initial instructions that make sure your agent uses them.
It starts from the moment you fire up your coding agent. As soon as it sees that you're building something, it doesn't just jump into trying to write code. Instead, it steps back and asks you what you're really trying to do.
Once it's teased a spec out of the conversation, it shows it to you in chunks short enough to actually read and digest.
After you've signed off on the design, your agent puts together an implementation plan that's clear enough for an enthusiastic junior engineer with poor taste, no judgement, no project context, and an aversion to testing to follow. It emphasizes true red/green TDD, YAGNI (You Aren't Gonna Need It), and DRY.
Next up, once you say "go", it launches a subagent-driven-development process, having agents work through each engineering task, inspecting and reviewing their work, and continuing forward. It's not uncommon for your agent to work autonomously for a couple hours at a time without deviating from the plan you put together.
There's a bunch more to it, but that's the core of the system. And because the skills trigger automatically, you don't need to do anything special. Your coding agent just has Superpowers.

These are some technical tips for publishing a blog. These have nothing to do with good content, just how to share that content. The recommendations are roughly in order of importance and have rationale for why they are that important.
Formats
People generally call feeds âRSS Feedsâ but usually they arenât specifically talking about RSS. RSS isnât the only, or even the best format. Using a standardized format is critical to your feed being understood by the widest variety of readers and search engines.

If you donât have a feed yet I would highly recommend Atom. The specification has much less ambiguity, so you are less likely to have compatibility issues with the wide variety of clients in use. The specification is also simpler and more clear overall. If you already have an RSS 2 feed there is little reason to upgrade.

		<name>{{AUTHOR_NAME}}</name>
	</author>

		<title>{{ENTRY.TITLE}}</title>

		<id>{{ENTRY.PERMALINK}}</id>

- JSON Feed: Content-Type: application/feed+json
You will see other values used in the wild, but these are the standard values and have the widest support.

Every URL in your feed should be absolute. While Atom has clearly specified how to resolve relative URLs they are rarely implemented correctly. In order to ensure that your feed can be understood by all readers use only absolute URLs (starting with https://).

On all of your blog pages and likely every page of your site you should include metadata to advertise you feed. This will allow readers and search engines to subscribe and become aware of your new content. This is as simple as providing the following in your HTML:

<link rel=alternate title="Comments on this Post" type=application/atom+xml href="/post/hello-world/comments.atom">

By David Buchanan (aka retr0id), 2nd October 2026

You can confirm that those are the winning lottery numbers, shown hours before the draw time, at https://www.euro-millions.com/results/28-08-2026.
Background
A typical C2PA manifest contains two signatures. The first is the "claim" signature, and in the case of a camera app the claim might be something like "this is a captured photograph, taken at these GPS coordinates, at this time" (except expressed more formally, per the C2PA spec).
In my previous article I showed that the claim signature is approximately worthless, and we can sign whatever claims we like (at least, we can in the case of flagship C2PA implementations like the Google Pixel Camera app).
But there's usually a second signature, from a Time Stamp Authority (TSA), and this one is more interesting. We don't trust devices to tell their own time, so instead a remote server is consulted, via the RFC 3161 protocol. The server sends back a signature that asserts "yes, I saw this hash at this timestamp", and this response is embedded into the C2PA metadata. As long as we trust that the server isn't misbehaving, this proves that the data being signed existed at-or-before the specified timestamp. The TSA acts like an independent witness.
For the Pixel 10, Google decided that devices can in fact be trusted to tell their own time. I have not evaluated their "on-device trusted time-stamp" implementation yet, but I'm highly sceptical of it.
Despite this, in this article we will not be attacking the TSA: we assume it functions as advertised.
It's Time-Hacking Time
If the TSA mechanism is secure, how are we going to hack time?
We're going to use my favourite bug class: the spec footgun.
The footgun is as follows: C2PA allows for arbitrary "exclusions". These are byte ranges within the file which are excluded from signature calculations. Yup. Really. This is already known (it's literally in the spec), but for some reason nobody's done anything about it yet.
In fact, Dr. Neal Krawertz explicitly called it out in his "Big Bulleted List" of C2PA flaws published June 2025:
- Large exclusion range. The manifest typically excludes a very large byte range from the signatures. Any excluded bytes can be altered without detection.
The only new angle here is that a malicious signer can deliberately use a large exclusion range, rather than merely doing so incidentally. We can exclude the entire file, to produce an entirely valid signature over an empty string. This allows the file to be tampered with after the fact, without invalidating the signature, and without invalidating the TSA's timestamp proof.
Time status: Hacked
So I really did take a picture of a lottery ticket, and attach a valid C2PA signature with a valid trusted timestamp. But I crafted the manifest to exclude the whole file, allowing me to photoshop it (poorly) after the numbers were announced, without invalidating any of the signatures.
Using c2patool -d to dump the manifest of my PoC file, we can see the important part:
3995383 is the length of the entire file, and 47DE...uFU= is the hash of an empty string:
$ openssl sha256 -binary /dev/null | base64
47DEQpj8HBSa+/TImW+5JCeuQeRkm5NMpJWZG3hSuFU=
The claim signature is over that hash, and the timestamp signature is over the claim signature. We're effectively signing nothing at all, but as of today all the C2PA verification tools I can find don't flag anything as unusual.
Can it be fixed?
Not easily! Sure, it's trivial to detect when the entire file has been excluded and report it as invalid, but what if only a small part is excluded? How do you tell whether it's something harmless, or something that could completely change the appearance of the image if modified? (See MD5 hash collision PoCs for examples of the latter).
My first draft of this post ended here with "My recommendation is that the exclusion feature should be excluded from the C2PA spec," but it's not that simple!
The main reason exclusions exist in the first place is that certain file formats effectively require it. For example in PNG files, each chunk has a CRC32 checksum, which needs to be corrected after the signature has been embedded into the file. This would create a circular dependency, unless the CRC32 is excluded from the signature's coverage (ignoring clever mathematical tricks that could avoid invalidating the CRC).
With that in mind I think the right solution here is to carefully and explicitly specify which parts of a file are allowed to be excluded, for each supported file format, and require that verifiers enforce these constraints.

Clip Pro 共有三种配色可选，似乎是去掉了在其他款式中常见的那个浅薄荷绿，耳夹这边保留了标志性的珊瑚橙色以及两种不同灰度的灰色。我们自然是挑了最具品牌气息的橙色款入手，它其实还是基于两种不同饱和度的橙色实现的。亮面部分比如充电盒内腔、耳机本体的前后模块都是饱和度更高的状态，盒外侧以及耳夹 C 桥部分是细磨砂处理或者亲肤硅胶层，相对会浅一点。这种珊瑚橙在大多数灯光下都有点三文鱼的感觉，并不会特别扎眼但还是足够鲜艳的。
充电盒整体非常小巧，不会比常规的 TWS 耳塞更大，顶盖上仍然保留了 CMF 耳塞产品的那个大旋钮，这也是通体唯一不是橙色的部分。这个旋钮正是耳机配对按键，以前 CMF 部分产品这个旋钮还可以实现其他功能，但目前在 Clip Pro 上默认是把旋转操作禁用的，在 app 端开启它之前，旋钮只是一个有段落感的解压玩具而已。同时或许是由于这个旋钮外露的比例比以前真无线产品更多，你在把玩旋转的时候会觉得它会出现倾斜，玩着玩着就卡一下，解压变增压……
USB 口位于背部，单手开盖还是非常轻松的，转轴虽然不是全金属，但反馈手感还算明确。耳机本体在盒内的放置方式有点怪，是后端模块朝下的交叉排布，如果你见过 FreeClip 2 的那种放置状态应该就会明白。耳机本体是分左右的，我们实测左右调换也不可以充电（磁铁极性导致你都放不进去，但磁吸力度足够，不用担心细节。
耳机本体我称之为「罗锅化」的耳夹形态，大体上仍然是可以视为前后模块 + C 桥的结构，但 C 桥跟前腔之间的固定并非常见耳夹的样子，而是加上了一个角度，这让前腔看上去会进一步往内弯，出音口的位置也会往里，你可以理解为做了更对准耳道口的结构。后端模块是比较规矩的圆柱形，从某些角度看上去甚至像是以前那个「垃圾桶」形态的 Mac Pro…… 但仔细去看，与耳廓接触的部分还是有轻微的曲面下凹，并非没做贴合结构。
这套设计使得佩戴的友好程度其实还不错，你也不必担心它会有低价耳夹常见的 C 桥偏硬的情况，前后模块之间的夹力较轻，且由于 C 桥长度足够，即使是厚耳廓也不用特别担心舒适度。这种结构也方便你一次性就佩戴好它，不需要频繁调整佩戴角度。
耳机本体支持 IP54 防尘防泼溅，应对基本的小雨天气没有问题，充电盒也支持 IPX2（虽然官网写的是「防尘」但 IPX2 对应的其实是防水等级，或许是标注错误）。
操作、软件功能与通话 | Control, APP & Call
Clip Pro 的操作全部依靠后端模块顶部的实体按键实现，这也算是某种物理盲操设计，可以实现对于单击、双击、长按以及点击后再长按的动作识别，默认的逻辑是单击播放控制、双击/三击切歌、长按控制音量，较为直观，除了单次按压以外的操作均可以自定义。按压反馈的灵敏度不必担心，毕竟是实体按键，但提示音在一些连击操作下会有一秒左右的迟滞。音量适中，佩戴识别功能是没有的。
Nothing X app 依然保持了耳机设置类 app 中较为直观的 UI 逻辑，提供了寻找耳机、固件更新、调音和设置自定义等功能。寻找耳机依然是基于耳机本体发出比较高音量的连续提示音实现，响度不算很高，在安静室内相对可用但咖啡厅之类的嘈杂环境就不太够了。关于各项声音相关功能我们会在后文单开一段介绍。
Clip Pro 搭载了单侧双麦克风、全机身共四颗麦克风，全部用于通话收音与降噪。我们实际在运营商网络下进行通话测试，它实际通话稳定性还不错，响度适中，清晰度中规中矩，会有比较明显的失真。在环境噪音较复杂或者风噪影响下，能听到比较明显的算法介入。三个方向的风源都不会产生明显的影响，硬要说的来自后方的风源会有更明显的介入痕迹，但总体上通话抗风噪的效果还是不错的。
信号与续航 | Connection & Battery
作为少有支持 LDAC 的耳夹，我们自然是要依然对于它在两种模式下的信号进行主观测试。
信号优先/最佳效果模式连接标准测试设备 Xperia 1 V 的状态下，无论 WLAN 关闭还是开启的情况下，卡顿丢包情况都比较少。距离 7.5m 且隔承重墙的状态下也不会有卡顿的明显增加。超过 8m 的隔墙距离下才会出现丢包卡顿。
设备不变，切换至音质优先模式，则信号稳定范围来到隔墙 2m 以内、空旷 3m 以内。在 3~5m 的范围内虽然不断连不卡顿，但是会有丢包感，这个表现其实并不算出色。
在信号密集区域打开 LDAC 音质优先，卡顿丢包情况在手持手机行走的情况下时有出现，信号优先下则大多能够保持相对稳定的近场连接能力。日常使用或是非信号密集区域通勤时，建议信号优先/最佳效果，不建议切换到音质优先的高码率状态。
切换到 AAC 之后，无论 WLAN 开启与否，Xperia 1 V 与 iPhone 17 的测试结果都是足够稳定的，也能在保持相当距离的情况下确保信号流畅，在 10m 内的范围没什么问题，隔墙或更远就会有一定影响。
它也支持 Google Fast Pair 和 Windows Swift Pair，双设备连接与 LDAC 并不冲突。
延迟方面，它有独立的低延迟模式，且低延迟模式与 LDAC 没有冲突。实际感知上，无论是否开启低延迟、在以 LDAC 编解码器信号优先连接 Xperia 1 V 的状态，进行流媒体视频和本地视频观看，它的延迟都大概控制在正常语速少于半个字的程度，表现还可以。
续航方面，官方宣称单次连续播放时间 10h，搭配充电盒一共为 32.5h。它还有一个额外的长续航模式可选，并在此状态下总续航能来到 36h。个人经过标准流程测试下来，以 LDAC 编解码器连接 Sony Xperia 1 V，设置其他为默认，以 50% 音量连续播放流媒体音乐（Apple Music Lossless）、播客节目（小宇宙），仅耳机续航为 6 小时 46 分钟（三次标识满电状态下均值）。LDAC 功耗偏大，考虑到官方的测量条件可能为 AAC，且 AAC 连接下同等条件测试结果三次最低值也有 10 小时 37 分钟，可以认为符合甚至略微超过官方描述。
充电方面，我们依然进行了测试，它在初期可以一度达到接近 2W，后期则稳定在 0.4~0.5W， PD 支持没问题。
扬声器、声音模式、漏音控制与编解码器 | Driver, Sound Modes, Leak Control & Codec
Clip Pro 搭载了直径 10.8mm 的 TPU + LCP 振膜动圈单元，支持的编码是 SBC / AAC / LDAC。搭载了被称为 Malleus Algorithm 的算法，其实就是动态补偿。
Nothing X app 里提供了非常多的声音相关功能，我们都快速过一下。
空间音频其实并非 Dolby Atmos 标准，而是单纯的「空间音效」，对于人声的结像进行模糊化，显得头中效应减少，混响增多，仅此而已，没有常开的必要。低音增强共有两档，实际即使开启到最高档也并不会有轰头的情况，这个低音增强更多像是满足低音量下的额外补偿，而非以往 Nothing 耳机的明显加能量。
均衡器提供了一个自定义档位和五个预设。自定义档位下是三个频率点 ±6 档的调节，显然不具备 Headphone (1) 上的高级 PEQ。五种预设里我个人会建议尝试「增强人声」，在室外环境使用时对于一些短视频或播客场景中的人声信息凸出有正面作用。漏音抑制模式的实现方式有点像我们之前在 LinkBuds Clip 上见到的，对于高频有非常明显的衰减，对于音乐聆听没有什么好处，但的确在减少漏音中的中高频（也是主要会让别人听到的频段）上有明显作用。你可以单独选择在通话时开启，这个设计很好。
考虑到漏音抑制模式的特性，我们还是基于不开启它的状态来评价对于 OWS 耳机来说颇为重要的实际声压和漏音控制表现，标准状态 AAC 连接 iPhone 17 对比，跟这段时间新测的耳夹保持一致从而控制输出增益的变量（兴戈圆气、达音科 LUNA 以及马上要测的 vivo 新品等）。将系统软件音量控制在 50%，正常佩戴。就我个人来说，标准模式此时的播放音量，在安静室内聆听大部分以当下 remaster 标准电平歌曲时会觉得能听到细节，但是明显是比日常听音音量要低的，也要比圆气和 LUNA 同样示数下有所不足。在连接 Xperia 1 V 的时候，同样系统显示 50 % 时会因为 Xperia 的蓝牙增益较高达到足够聆听的音量，iPhone 17 就得到 70% 以上才能接近类似水平。由于不同系统、不同设备、不同听力状态都会对于你进行判断产生干扰，这点还是要注意。
漏音方面，还是 iPhone 50% 音量为准下在 10cm 开外仅有轻微感知，15cm 开外几乎不可感。防漏音表现即使放到旗舰耳夹里也算相当不错的水平，这点表现还不错，但只是部分因为声压并不算大导致的……
声音主观描述 | Sound Description
基于 LDAC 编码连接 Xperia 1 V，关闭低音增强、空间音频和漏音抑制，均衡器为自定义清零状态。
低频量感适中，厚度适中，饱满度不算多。弹性表现中规中矩，下潜能力在中价位表现还可以。收放速度适中，保留的残响较少。氛围烘托的晕染感和浓郁度都不多。Clip Pro 的低频总体上依然是比较干净的，相较于小米耳夹和一些数码品牌的中价位出品，Clip Pro 的低频质感会更加自然一些，不会显得过于凌厉，这点是把控得不错。基音位于中下盘的乐器没有前倾问题。
中频，人声的距离稍近，但不至于贴脸，精致程度适中。对于人声的质感表现略优先于线条刻画，厚度适中，线条的突出程度不会有额外强调。对于男女声之间的倾向不算明显，适合于不过于粗或者不偏锐利的声线类型，相对还算普适。颗粒感有所打磨，大体上还是比较平顺的。音色有轻微的暖感，但并不影响判断，大体上不会有明显的抓耳强调。喉音位置比较标准，气声比例适中，相较于不少入门耳夹，在丰富度上稍好。口水声等的前凸有所控制（不是完全磨平），齿音也比较平顺，不会有明显的耐听度问题。人声频段的通透度还可以，不至于有不自然的偏亮。
乐器方面，大部分乐器的呈现都是质感略优先于线条的。弦乐器中，小提琴、吉他、中提琴等的拉拨弦稍有凸出，但抓耳感不会过分。大提琴的形体感比较细，在空间里比例不多。铜管类的气势感不算足，需要亮度的小号等有相对多一点的亮感。木管类的自然度还可以，空气感稍有一点突出。乐器的泛音不算多，听上去较为精致。打击乐器中，Kick 的存在感不强，Snare 收得稍快，镲片类有足够亮度但金属感和刺激度还是有所控制的。
高频总体亮度适中，不会有明显的尖峰，整体还算比较平滑。中高频过渡区域有一定的厚度，极高频的延伸能力表现中规中矩，跟旗舰耳夹普遍水平比起来会在极高频信息量上缺点东西，滚降稍快稍早。
声场的规模不算大，横纵向距离都不会拉得很远，边界感的弥散度也不算高。结合中规中矩的「高度感」，Clip Pro 呈现出的是比较规矩的扁椭球形空间。人声与乐器之间的分离度在这个价位还可以，整体感没什么问题。解析能力中规中矩，有轻度的「解析感」强调。动态一般，瞬态还不错。
总结评价 | Overall Impression
CMF Clip Pro 作为产品的综合完成度其实还可以，保持了 CMF 作为 Nothing 子品牌在设计方面的一些优势，续航和佩戴体验等没有明显短板，声音大体上也在我们认为的合理区间，没有出现风格走向怪异的情况。不过，我们对于中价位的耳夹还是期望能够有更好的声压，在大多数移动设备 50% 难以满足安静环境听音响度需求的情况下，我们还是难以给它 V 级推荐，更何况在圆气、LUNA 这些声音上完成度明显更高的型号已经推出的前提下，它的优势会更少一些。虽然有高码率编码，但在音质优先的模式下连接稳定性还需要优化。好在，相较于部分手机品牌的耳夹，CMF 这边的溢价反倒显得克制一些了，甚至有的渠道能更低，这也是我们愿意给 IV 级评分的原因之一。

Redefining enterprise intelligence with autonomous AI
Composable infrastructure, sovereign data, and cross-functional coordination can enable intelligence to flow and AI to grow smarter.
In partnership withUniphore
Enterprise AI is no longer a future ambition. It is in full operational flight. Model capabilities are advancing faster than most organizations can absorb, while the cost of performance continues to fall. Globally, AI investment is set to reach $2.5 trillion in 2026, up 44% from the previous year.
For many enterprises, this investment has produced fragmentation. Intelligence can accumulate in silos so that sales agents are unaware of open support tickets, for instance, or marketing systems are personalizing content without visibility into what finance already knows about a customer. Each function may perform well in isolation, but the enterprise as a whole learns little and has less information to act upon.
The shift from AI as a tool to AI as an operating model—what we call the “agentic shift” in this report—demands something more fundamental than better models or faster infrastructure. It requires connecting people, processes, and data in real time, along with the governance and control to act on that intelligence reliably.
This means rethinking both architecture and operating models simultaneously. First, rebuilding data infrastructure for accessibility rather than volume. Second, replacing fixed tech stacks with composable architectures that can evolve as models and tools change. And, lastly, resolving questions of AI sovereignty, including where intelligence runs, who controls it, and how it operates across organizational and jurisdictional boundaries.
Key findings include the following:
Enterprise AI’s scaling problem is structural. Process-first companies are pulling ahead. Global AI spending is rising sharply and model capabilities are advancing faster than most organizations can integrate them. Yet the majority of enterprises are still not growing revenue through AI or fundamentally rethinking how they operate. The companies generating sustained returns share a common discipline. They treat process redesign as the work that precedes model selection, building for how the technology will evolve rather than retrofitting roles and workflows after deployment. For them, the agentic shift begins with the operating model.
Data readiness, not data abundance, is what makes AI compoundable. Most enterprises discover too late that having data and having AI-ready data are very different things. A sovereign, composable foundation—one that queries and prepares data where it resides, without migration or centralization—can convert raw data estates into intelligence that AI agents can act upon. As data residency laws, multicloud environments, and structural complexity make centralization increasingly impractical, sovereign control over where models run and data lives is what keeps that adaptability intact.

Don’t be fooled—LLMs don’t reason
Ten years after AlphaGo’s match against Go champion Lee Sedol, today’s AI still isn’t tapping into the machinery that made that win possible.
On an afternoon in Seoul in March 2016, I watched a program I helped build put a stone on the fifth line of a Go board in what looked like a gift to its human opponent. Move 37 in game two of the five-game match looked so absurd that some commentators thought it was a programming glitch.
It wasn’t. AlphaGo won the game, ultimately triumphing 4-1 over Lee Sedol, one of the greatest professional Go players of all time. “I thought AlphaGo was based on probability calculation and that it was merely a machine,” Lee said afterwards. “But when I saw this move, I changed my mind. Surely, AlphaGo is creative.”
When Deep Blue defeated then reigning world chess champion Garry Kasparov in 1997, it did so by looking six to eight moves ahead per player and evaluating 200 million chess positions per second, using rules hard-coded by humans. Go is a vastly more complex game. A stone’s worth depends on how distant groups and territory unfold over dozens of moves. Computing even a fraction of the possible outcomes would take a supercomputer billions of years. To win, AlphaGo had to sense who was ahead at a glance and even invent moves no human had thought to play.
That is why many accounts of AlphaGo’s match against Lee portray move 37 as a flash of pure machine intuition. But that is a misunderstanding. It was actually AlphaGo’s powers of reasoning that made this creative choice—and these are powers that today’s AI lacks. If we want future AI systems to produce trustworthy results and really novel insights in fields like science and medicine, we need to equip them with genuine reasoning capabilities of this kind.
AlphaGo is made up of two systems. The first, its policy network, was trained to guess what move a strong human would play. This “intuitive” part regarded move 37 as nothing special—a play that had a roughly one in 10,000 chance of being made by an expert human player. What made AlphaGo choose it was the program’s search machinery, which looked beyond immediate plausibility and weighed the future consequences of proposed moves. It explicitly constructed and searched a game tree with thousands of branches, each representing a different possible future.
A well-known theory in the behavioral sciences, popularized by Daniel Kahneman, distinguishes between two modes of human thought: System 1 is fast, gut-level, effortless; system 2, slow, step-by-step, and deliberative. AlphaGo offered a striking machine analogue of that split. Its networks supplied the hunches—this move looks promising, this position looks won—and its search supplied the deliberation, testing those hunches against the moves and countermoves that would follow. As in human cognition, neither half works alone. Intuition alone would never have opted for move 37, and brute-force search would have struggled to sieve through all the many possible moves.
This is strikingly different from the way today’s AI models work. A large language model picks the next token, over and over. That amounts to system 1 in action—fast, associative, and surprisingly good pattern completion across almost every subject people write about.
Not long after ChatGPT debuted, the field realized that language fluency alone falls short of true usefulness. The apparent solution was to make models that deliberate: Instead of answering immediately, they can now generate intermediate steps that decompose a problem, carry forward partial results, and influence subsequent reasoning—a process known as chain of thought.
The gains have proved real, above all in mathematics and coding. But unlike AlphaGo’s search, this does not introduce a genuinely separate reasoning mechanism: The intermediate reasoning is still produced by the same next-token prediction process, iterated for longer before the model commits to an answer.
Three shortcomings prevent what chatbots do from qualifying as reasoning (in a way that a scientist might recognize). First, these models typically maintain no explicit, persistent, and inspectable epistemic state. There is no open ledger that lays out the hypotheses a model is considering, the confidence it has in various explanations, the evidence it’s weighing, and the unresolved questions it’s holding onto—all things that should be systematically revised as new information arrives.
Second, they lack a clean separation between what the system knows and how it manipulates that knowledge. Knowledge and reasoning are inextricably interwoven in the weights of the neural network—there is no independent, explicitly represented set of beliefs. Third, while the chains of thought chatbots produce look like deliberation, research has demonstrated that the bots often concoct them after the fact, reaching an answer by one route but reporting another.
This is a problem because in the high-stakes applications we all care about, such as medicine, engineering, and scientific research, it matters not only what a system concludes but also how it arrives at its conclusion. When mistakes happen—for example, in medical diagnosis and treatment—we need to be able to pinpoint what went wrong: Was the system’s reasoning at fault, did it draw on invalid evidence, or did it make incorrect assumptions?
This is why I recently left my position at Google DeepMind. I believe we need a fresh approach to machine reasoning—one that draws on AlphaGo’s architecture. AlphaGo maintains a record of what it knows about a given position: the game tree. This data structure contains all the variations, the possible futures, that AlphaGo has considered, each move and position being annotated with judgments made by its neural networks. As its reasoning progresses, AlphaGo updates the game tree and eventually synthesizes the information in it to decide which move to make.

Hate speech — discourse that attacks a person or group on the basis of their inherent characteristics or identity — is on the rise worldwide. It can be triggered by various events, from elections and migrant crises to this year’s men’s football World Cup. Posts that were hostile to people of South Asian origin in the United States rose by 240% on the social-media platform X between January 2024 and June this year.
Hateful discourse can widen social divisions, instill ‘us versus them’ narratives and increase political polarization. Divisive rhetoric is also being exacerbated by content generated by artificial-intelligence models and bot accounts.
Scientists are keen to understand these phenomena so that they can find the most effective ways to counter them. Mirta Galesic, a co-leader of the research group Collective Minds at the Complexity Science Hub in Vienna, studies hate speech and anti-hate speech, called counterspeech. Galesic talks to Nature about strategies to tackle hateful discourse online.
Why is it an important time to study hate speech?
In some countries, polarization is quite prominent — especially ‘affective polarization’, when individuals dislike people who have different political opinions to their own. Rising inequality, shifts in power, armed conflicts and institutions not working as well as they were before create uncertainty. The accumulation of this uncertainty is easily channelled into hate towards particular groups of people.
And given how easy it is to lead people into thinking the worst about others — especially now, by using AI tools to create fake photos and videos — I would say that it’s extremely important to study hate speech. Everyone should be able to recognize their own susceptibility to be manipulated.
How do you research hate speech?
My colleagues and I analysed more than one million posts made between January 2015 and December 2018 on the social-media site Twitter (now known as X). These posts were responses to roughly 130,000 posts by German news organizations, journalists, bloggers and politicians. This time period started with what is sometimes referred to as the ‘migrant crisis’ in Germany, when more than one million refugees and asylum seekers moved there after a policy change allowed them to apply to stay in the nation from Germany, rather than from the first European Union country they arrived in.
In subsequent studies, we used large language models to measure hate in conversations. We also categorized numerous ways in which posters responded to each other and identified which strategies were most effective at countering hate.
What did you find were the best ways to counter hate?
If you see hate speech online, take it almost as your duty to disagree. You just say ‘I disagree’. Don’t feel pressured to have an elaborate argument. Just express your dissent — not to change the mind of the commenter, but to show people that others have a different opinion.
Next, ask friends to join and say that they also disagree.
Lastly, if you encounter a person who has an opinion that is different to yours, don’t cut them off. Try to learn more about them and find out what you share with them. You will be able to get through to them more easily after that.
Often, people are in situations in which it’s easy to encounter extreme examples online, and feelings of unease and fear can be amplified in online spaces. Humans are very social beings. We align ourselves to our environment. The more something is discussed, the more extreme people’s opinions tend to become. That’s why just showing up and saying ‘I disagree’ is so important.
